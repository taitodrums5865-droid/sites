8(a) certification requires a firm that is small under SBA size standards and at least 51% directly owned and controlled by socially and economically disadvantaged U.S. citizens. Each of those owners must have net worth of $850,000 or less, average AGI of $400,000 or less, and total assets of $6.5 million or less (13 CFR 124.104).

You also need good character and, in most cases, two full years of operating revenue (SBA). The biggest change in 2026: since September 10, 2026, no racial or ethnic group is presumed socially disadvantaged. Individual applicants now have to document discrimination against their group and self-certify that it materially harmed them (Holland & Knight).

Key points

  • Net worth cap is $850K, excluding home, business and IRA equity
  • Since Sept 10, 2026, no group is presumed socially disadvantaged
  • A denied firm can reapply after 90 days or appeal some denials in 45

Best for: Small business owners deciding whether to apply for SBA 8(a)

What Is 8(a) Certification?

The SBA 8(a) Business Development program certifies small businesses owned by socially and economically disadvantaged individuals. Certified firms can win sole-source and set-aside federal contracts for up to 9 years. It is a business development program with a fixed end date, and you only get one turn: SBA calls it one-time participation per individual over a lifetime (SBA).

The most valuable benefit is the sole-source award. Agencies can give an 8(a) firm a contract directly, without competition, up to set dollar thresholds. As of October 1, 2025, the FAR inflation adjustment raised those thresholds from $4.5 million to $5.5 million for most acquisitions and from $7 million to $8.5 million for manufacturing NAICS codes (Federal Register, Aug. 27, 2025; SmallGovCon). Above those amounts, contracts are normally competed among 8(a) firms. The exception is firms owned by Indian tribes or Alaska Native Corporations, where SBA can accept larger sole-source awards (FAR 19.805-1).

For example, a two-person IT services firm in the program could take a $3 million help-desk requirement as a sole-source award, because it falls under the $5.5 million non-manufacturing threshold. If that same requirement were $6 million, it would normally be competed among 8(a) firms instead.

You should also know the program has been under heavy scrutiny in 2026. In January, SBA suspended 1,091 participants, about 25% of the roughly 4,300 firms in the program, for missing a January 19, 2026 deadline to submit three years of financial documents (SBA news release 26-26). In March it moved to terminate more than 620 firms (SBA news release 26-34). If you join, assume SBA will ask for clean financial records and will expect them on time.

Work through the checklist in this order, because the cheapest checks come first:

  1. Look up your size standard. Find your primary NAICS code and compare your average receipts or employee count to SBA's size standard. If you fail here, nothing else matters.
  2. Read your own operating agreement or bylaws. Look for anything that gives a non-disadvantaged person veto power, supermajority votes, or the right to block decisions. Under 13 CFR 124.106, a disadvantaged owner of a corporation controls the board through 100% ownership, or 51%+ voting stock plus a board seat, provided no supermajority requirements get in the way.
  3. Run the three financial tests (next section) for each disadvantaged owner.
  4. Pull two years of business tax returns and confirm both show operating revenue.
  5. Start collecting social disadvantage evidence. It takes the longest, so begin it in parallel with steps 1–4.

Pitfall: ownership through a holding company fails the "directly owned" test. Fix: restructure so the individual holds the shares directly before you apply, and keep the transfer documents.

What Are the Net Worth, Income, and Asset Limits for 8(a)?

SBA caps adjusted net worth at $850,000, three-year average AGI at $400,000, and total assets at $6.5 million. Each cap has its own exclusions, and applicants often apply the exclusions to the wrong test (13 CFR 124.104, as of October 2026).

What Are the Net Worth, Income, and Asset Limits for 8(a)?. Source: SAM.gov
TestLimitExcludedNot excluded (common miss)
Net worth≤ $850,000Your ownership interest in the applicant firm; equity in your primary residence; funds in an IRA or other official retirement accountEquity in rental property, a second home, or other businesses
Income (AGI)≤ $400,000, averaged over the 3 preceding yearsPass-through income from an S corp, LLC or partnership that was reinvested in the businessSalary, distributions you took out, investment income
Total assets≤ $6.5 million fair market valueOnly qualified IRA/retirement fundsYour home and your business interest, both of which do count here

Sources: Cornell LII, 13 CFR 124.104; thresholds confirmed in Federal Register 2023-15078.

The total-assets test is the one people get wrong most easily. Your home and your company are excluded from net worth but included in total assets. For example (illustrative numbers only), an owner might have a $1.4 million home, a business valued at $4 million, a $700,000 rental property, and $500,000 in a 401(k). Net worth counts only the rental equity, which could be well under $850,000. Total assets count $1.4M + $4M + $0.7M = $6.1 million, a pass but with little room left. If the business value rises to $4.5 million, the owner fails.

Some other rules from 13 CFR 124.104 catch people out:

  • The home-equity exclusion has a carve-out. Equity that came from excessive withdrawals from the business is not excluded.
  • Retirement accounts need paperwork. You have to tell SBA the terms and restrictions of the account and certify that it is legitimate (eCFR).
  • Gifts to family within two years count against you. Assets transferred to immediate family for less than fair market value in the two years before you apply are attributed back to you. The exceptions are transfers for education, medical expenses, essential support, and customary gifts such as birthdays or graduations.
  • Your spouse submits separate financial information unless you are legally separated. SBA only counts the spouse's finances if the spouse plays a role in the business or has financially supported it.
  • The $400,000 AGI test is a rebuttable presumption, not an absolute bar. If a one-time event pushed you over, you can explain it. Expect to need documents that back up your explanation.

Fix for income spikes: if one year's AGI is high because of reinvested pass-through income, show it line by line using your K-1s and the business's retained earnings. That reinvested amount is exactly what the regulation excludes.

What Counts as 'Socially and Economically Disadvantaged' for 8(a)?

As of September 10, 2026, no group is presumed socially disadvantaged for individually owned firms. Every new individual applicant has to show discrimination against their group and self-certify that it materially harmed them. You must also pass the economic tests above. SBA published this final rule on August 11, 2026 (Federal Register 2026-16370; NASBP).

The new test has two parts (Holland & Knight; Covington):

  1. Group discrimination, backed by evidence. You show that a governmental or private entity "discriminated against, disfavored, or otherwise disadvantaged an identifiable racial, ethnic, or cultural group" that you belong to. Acceptable evidence includes government, university or corporate documents and policies, official statements, court decisions and administrative rulings, congressional findings, and "other adequate evidence."
  2. Membership and material harm, self-certified. You certify that you belong to the group and that you suffered "loss of access to or diminished opportunities related to economic advancement." The final rule says this can include being dissuaded from applying to a program because of barriers your group faces.

According to Holland & Knight's summary, the final rule clarifies that discrimination based on sex and disability can qualify. USFCR reports that the old narrative essay, the list of presumed groups, and the preponderance-of-evidence standard are gone (USFCR).

Who the change does not affect:

  • Entity-owned firms owned by Alaska Native Corporations, Community Development Corporations, Indian Tribes or Native Hawaiian Organizations.
  • Current individually owned participants, who do not have to re-establish social disadvantage.

What to do now:

  1. Name the specific group you belong to.
  2. Collect published sources showing that an institution's policy or practice disadvantaged that group. Court rulings, agency findings and congressional findings carry the most weight because the rule lists them by name.
  3. Write a short factual statement connecting that discrimination to a concrete loss of economic opportunity for you. For example, you might describe a lending or bonding barrier, or a program you were steered away from. Keep it factual and avoid adjectives.
  4. Keep copies of every source with its URL and date.

Pitfall: writing a personal-hardship essay in the old style. The new rule is built around evidence of group-level discrimination, so an essay about individual setbacks alone answers the wrong question. Fix: lead with the documented institutional evidence, then add your material-harm certification. As of USFCR's September 2026 summary, SBA had not published detailed templates, so check the SBA 8(a) page for guidance before you submit.

How to Apply for 8(a) Certification on Certify.gov

You apply through MySBA Certifications at certifications.sba.gov (people often call it "Certify.gov"). Before you start, you need an active SAM.gov registration and your UEI, and SBA has 90 days to process a complete application (SBA).

  1. Identify your primary NAICS code(s). Your size standard depends on it.
  2. Register or renew in SAM.gov. MySBA Certifications needs your Unique Entity Identifier (UEI) and Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN), plus a CAGE Code if you have one (MySBA Certifications FAQ).
  3. Take the eligibility questionnaire at SBA Certifications for a preliminary read on whether 8(a) fits you.
  4. Sign in at certifications.sba.gov and choose your role: Primary Qualified Owner (PQO) if you are the disadvantaged owner, or contributor if you are someone else adding information (such as a spouse or another owner) (MySBA onboarding search summary).
  5. Claim your business by entering the UEI, CAGE Code (if applicable) and TIN. Make sure they match SAM exactly. After onboarding you land on your dashboard.
  6. Select 8(a) Business Development. The platform lets you apply for several certifications at once, such as 8(a) together with WOSB or HUBZone (FAQ).
  7. Upload documents covering ownership (operating agreement or bylaws, stock certificates), control (resumes, officer roles), finances (personal and business tax returns, personal financial statements for each disadvantaged owner and any required spouse), and your social disadvantage evidence.
  8. Submit and watch your dashboard and email. If SBA finds the application incomplete, it returns it for edits through email and in-app notifications. Add management+ucp@certify.sba.gov to your approved senders so those messages reach you (FAQ).

When you get a returned file, rebuild the social disadvantage section first and keep your financials current.

Once you're certified, agencies will still judge you on how you present yourself. A short, specific capability statement is what contracting officers read when they consider you for a sole-source award.

Top Reasons 8(a) Applications Get Rejected

The regulations make some gates far easier to fail than others: control and ownership, missing financial documents, and the financial caps. Your business idea itself is rarely the problem. SBA doesn't publish a ranked list of denial reasons, so the list below is drawn from the rules themselves.

1. Control problems. The disadvantaged owner keeps a full-time job elsewhere, isn't the top officer, or a non-disadvantaged minority owner holds veto rights. A non-disadvantaged person also can't be paid more than the highest-ranking officer unless that's commercially reasonable (13 CFR 124.106). Fix: leave the outside job, amend the operating agreement, and document how pay is set.

2. Indirect ownership. Shares held through a holding LLC or an irrevocable trust fail the "directly owned" requirement (13 CFR 124.105). Fix: move the shares into the individual's name.

3. No two-year history and a weak waiver request. A waiver requires all five conditions: substantial management experience, technical experience, adequate capital, a record of successful contract performance, and the people, facilities and equipment to do the work (13 CFR 124.107). Fix: if you can't document all five, wait until you have two years of returns showing operating revenue.

4. Financial cap miscalculations. These include counting the home in net worth (it's excluded), leaving it out of total assets (it's included), or ignoring gifts to family in the last two years. Fix: rebuild the personal financial statement using the table above.

5. Prior use or family conflicts. If an immediate family member is using or has used disadvantaged status for another 8(a) firm, and the two firms share ownership, facilities or non-arm's-length contracts, or operate in the same industry without relevant experience, you can be blocked (13 CFR 124.105).

6. Social disadvantage evidence that answers the old question. Under the 2026 rule, personal hardship alone doesn't establish group-level discrimination.

What happens after a denial:

  • There is no reconsideration for declined applications (13 CFR 124.205).
  • Appeal to SBA's Office of Hearings and Appeals within 45 days. This is only allowed if the denial rests solely on social disadvantage, economic disadvantage, ownership, control, or a mix of those four (13 CFR 124.206). A denial based partly on anything else, such as potential for success, is final.
  • Reapply any time 90 calendar days after the final decision (13 CFR 124.207).

For example, a firm denied only because its owner works a second job at a hospital can't fix that on appeal, because the facts were true when SBA decided. The better route is to leave the job, wait 90 days, and reapply.

8(a) vs HUBZone vs WOSB: Which Certification Fits Your Business?

8(a) is for disadvantaged owners and lasts at most 9 years. HUBZone is based on where your office and employees are, and WOSB on women's ownership. Some businesses qualify for more than one, and MySBA Certifications lets you apply for several together.

Here's how to choose. If you pass the 8(a) financial caps but expect trouble with the new social disadvantage evidence, look at EDWOSB if you're a woman owner, since it uses the same financial limits without the social disadvantage test. If your office is already in a HUBZone, that certification doesn't run out after 9 years. WOSB set-asides only apply in NAICS codes where SBA has found women-owned firms underrepresented, so check the eligible NAICS list before counting on that program.

None of these programs replaces ordinary small business set-asides, which don't require any special certification. Our total vs partial set-aside checklist explains how those are structured.

How Long Does 8(a) Certification Last and What Happens After 9 Years?

8(a) certification lasts a maximum of nine years, starting from the date of SBA's approval letter. It is split into a 4-year developmental stage and a 5-year transitional stage, and when it ends you graduate and lose 8(a) benefits (13 CFR 124.2; SBA).

The term can only end early through termination, early graduation (including voluntary early graduation), or voluntary withdrawal (13 CFR 124.2). Staying in requires an annual review showing continuing eligibility (SBA). The 2026 suspensions showed what happens when firms don't produce records on request (SBA 26-26).

How to use the nine years:

  1. Years 1–4 (developmental): win your first sole-source awards, build past performance, and track every agency contact.
  2. Years 5–9 (transitional): move toward competitive work, and answer sources sought notices as a small business, not only as an 8(a) firm.
  3. Before graduating: decide whether HUBZone, WOSB or ordinary small business set-asides will carry you once 8(a) ends.
  4. Every year: keep three years of business and personal financial records ready to hand over.

Next steps

You can check most of the 8(a) gates yourself today, and free help is available if a gate is unclear.

Do it yourself today:

  1. Fill out a personal financial statement for each disadvantaged owner and run all three tests from the table above: $850K net worth, $400K average AGI, $6.5M total assets.
  2. Confirm your SAM.gov registration is active and that your UEI, CAGE and TIN match, then take the eligibility questionnaire at SBA Certifications.
  3. Start a folder for social disadvantage evidence, and record each court ruling, agency finding or policy document with its URL and date.

If you'd rather not do it alone:

  • APEX Accelerators (formerly PTACs) are funded by the Department of Defense and offer one-on-one certification guidance at no cost (as of October 2026; check the official page). There are more than 90 centers and 300+ local offices, and you can find yours at apexaccelerators.us (SupplierDiversity.com overview). They suit first-time applicants who need someone to review a file. They are less suited to anyone facing an OHA appeal, because APEX counselors give guidance, not legal representation, and their help is not an SBA eligibility decision.
  • SBA's own help line is certifications@sba.gov or 866-SBA-HELP (866-722-4357) for portal and process questions, also free (as of October 2026; check the official page) (SBA HUBZone page). Don't expect it to coach you on your evidence strategy.
  • A government contracts attorney makes sense if your file has ownership or control complications, or if you're within the 45-day appeal window. Fees vary by firm and there is no official rate, so ask for a fixed quote. If your case is simple and your financials pass easily, you probably don't need one.

FAQ

What is 8(a) certification used for in federal contracting?

It lets agencies award contracts to your firm without competition, up to $5.5 million for most acquisitions and $8.5 million for manufacturing (as of October 2025). It also lets you compete for contracts set aside only for 8(a) firms. The certification lasts up to nine years and includes SBA business development support (FAR inflation adjustment).

What is the net worth limit to qualify for 8(a) certification?

The limit is $850,000 in personal net worth for each disadvantaged owner. Your ownership interest in the applicant firm, equity in your primary residence, and funds in official retirement accounts are excluded. Assets you gave to immediate family below fair market value in the past two years can be counted back against you (13 CFR 124.104).

How long does the 8(a) certification application take to process?

SBA says it has 90 days to process a complete application (SBA). The clock effectively restarts if SBA returns your file as incomplete. Applications pending on September 10, 2026 were returned for resubmission under the new social disadvantage rule, so those applicants are starting again.

Can a business be both 8(a) and WOSB certified?

Yes. A woman-owned firm that meets both programs' requirements can hold both, and MySBA Certifications allows several certifications through one application (FAQ). EDWOSB uses the same $850K / $400K / $6.5M financial limits as 8(a) but doesn't require the social disadvantage showing.
