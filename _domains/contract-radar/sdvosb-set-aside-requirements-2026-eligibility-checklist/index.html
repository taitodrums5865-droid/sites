SDVOSB Set Aside Requirements: 2026 Eligibility Checklist

By Aoba Works · Published October 22, 2026 · 17 min read

How this guide was made: drafted with AI assistance from the official sources linked below, then passed an automated editorial check before publishing. Our process

  • Federal Contracting

To win an SDVOSB set-aside, your firm has to pass four tests. It must be small under the NAICS code in the solicitation. It must be at least 51% unconditionally and directly owned by one or more service-disabled veterans who live in the United States. Those veterans must also control the company. And SBA must certify the firm through its VetCert program, because self-certification no longer qualifies a firm for these awards (13 CFR 128.200; FAR 19.1405). Before award, the contracting officer checks that you appear in SAM as an SBA-certified SDVOSB. Then they apply the set-aside rules of the buying agency: FAR Subpart 19.14 for most agencies, or VAAR Part 819 for the Department of Veterans Affairs.

Key points

  • Service-disabled veterans must own at least 51% unconditionally
  • VetCert certification lasts 3 years; recertify in the last 90 days
  • VA sole-source cap is $5M; FAR caps are $5M or $8.5M (manufacturing)

Best for: Veteran business owners bidding on their first SDVOSB set-aside

What Are the SDVOSB Ownership Requirements?

At least 51% of the business must be unconditionally and directly owned by one or more service-disabled veterans. No condition, agreement, or trust can weaken that ownership (13 CFR 128.202).

"Unconditional" means the veteran's ownership is not subject to "any conditions, executory agreements, voting trusts, restrictions on or assignments of voting rights, or other arrangements" that could shift the benefits of ownership to someone else (13 CFR 128.202). "Direct" means the veteran holds the shares or membership interest personally. Ownership through another company or a trust, including an ESOP, does not count. The one exception is a revocable living trust where the qualifying veteran is the grantor, a trustee, and a current beneficiary.

The test also depends on your entity type:

What Are the SDVOSB Ownership Requirements?. Source: SAM.gov
Entity typeWhat the 51% must cover
LLCAt least 51% of each class of member interest
CorporationAt least 51% of all outstanding stock and 51% of each class of voting stock
PartnershipAt least 51% of every class of partnership interest, and a qualifying veteran must be a general partner controlling partnership decisions

Source: 13 CFR 128.202.

The rule also covers profits. Qualifying veterans must receive at least 51% of any profit distribution, in line with their ownership share. SBA ignores community property laws when it measures ownership. So a non-veteran spouse's community property interest does not lower the veteran's percentage on paper.

Steps to check your ownership before you apply

  1. Pull your current operating agreement, bylaws, or partnership agreement, plus every amendment.
  2. List every class of interest (voting, non-voting, preferred). Confirm the veteran holds 51% or more of each class, not just in total.
  3. Look for buy-sell, option, or voting agreements that could move control of the veteran's interest to someone else.
  4. Check the distribution clause. Profits must flow to the veteran in proportion to ownership.

Common pitfalls and fixes

  • Pitfall: A non-veteran investor holds preferred units with a veto over distributions. Fix: Remove the veto or convert the units before applying. Arrangements that move ownership benefits away from the veteran can break the "unconditional" test.
  • Pitfall: You assume a right of first refusal disqualifies you. Fix: It does not, as long as its terms follow normal commercial practice (13 CFR 128.202).
  • Pitfall: The veteran's shares sit in a holding LLC. Fix: Hold them personally or through a qualifying revocable living trust.

Example (illustration only): A veteran owns 60% of an LLC's Class A voting units. A non-veteran partner owns 100% of the Class B economic units. This fails, because the veteran must own 51% of each class.

The rule also protects surviving spouses. If the veteran dies, eligibility continues until the earliest of these: the spouse remarries, the spouse gives up ownership, 10 years pass (if the veteran was rated 100% disabled), or 3 years pass (if the rating was below 100%) (13 CFR 128.202).

What Are the SDVOSB Certification Requirements for 2026?

Since January 1, 2023, SBA certifies SDVOSBs through VetCert. SBA verifies veteran and disability status, ownership, control, and size before the firm can win set-asides at the VA or any other agency (SBA).

The 2021 National Defense Authorization Act moved certification from the VA to SBA as of January 1, 2023 (SBA). To qualify, the firm must be "identified by the VA as a Veteran or Service-Disabled Veteran." It must be small under the size standard for its NAICS code, and it must meet the 51% ownership and control tests (SBA veteran contracting programs). SBA also checks two other things. The firm and its owners cannot have an active exclusion in SAM. They also cannot be delinquent on significant federal debts, such as tax liens or loan defaults, unless they are current on an approved repayment plan (13 CFR 128.201).

Official numbers (as of October 2026; check the official pages)

  • Certification period: 3 years. You can recertify within the 90 calendar days before your eligibility ends. If you miss the date, SBA decertifies the firm, but you can be reinstated if you recertify within 30 days (13 CFR 128.306).
  • Change reporting: Tell SBA about eligibility changes within 30 calendar days (13 CFR 128.306).
  • Processing time: SBA reported in November 2025 that average review time had dropped to 12 days, down from 81 days at the end of 2024, and that the backlog of 2,700+ pending applications was cleared (SBA, Nov. 11, 2025).
  • Reapplying after a denial: Wait 90 calendar days from the decline date (13 CFR 128.305).
  • Help line: 866-SBA-HELP (866-722-4357), or certifications@sba.gov (SBA).

Steps to apply

  1. Make sure your SAM.gov registration is active and that you have your Unique Entity ID (UEI).
  2. Sign in to MySBA Certifications at certifications.sba.gov with your Login.gov account. Create a Login.gov account first if you don't have one.
  3. Choose the Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business certification and work through the ownership, control, and veteran-status sections.
  4. Upload your formation and governing documents, ownership records, and anything else SBA asks for. SBA keeps the minimum document list on its website (13 CFR 128.303). If you're already certified as 8(a) or WOSB, you may be able to reuse those documents.
  5. Watch your email and answer document requests quickly. If you don't respond adequately, SBA can deny the application for that reason (13 CFR 128.302).

Pitfalls and fixes

  • A denial for lack of veteran-status evidence cannot be appealed to SBA's Office of Hearings and Appeals. Other denials can be (13 CFR 128.304). Fix: Settle any discrepancy in your VA records with the VA before you apply.
  • After a denial, update SAM. If you don't appeal, you must update your SAM status within 10 business days of the denial. If you appeal to OHA, update it immediately (13 CFR 128.302).

What Is a Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business Set-Aside?

An SDVOSB set-aside is a federal contract that only certified SDVOSBs can bid on. These firms must be majority owned and controlled by veterans with a VA-rated service-connected disability.

The rules sit in FAR Subpart 19.14 for most agencies and in VAAR Part 819 for the VA. A contracting officer can reserve the whole contract for SDVOSBs. If only one capable SDVOSB exists, they can award it sole-source within dollar limits. Under FAR 19.1406, sole-source awards are capped at $8.5 million for manufacturing NAICS codes and $5 million for all other codes, including options. The officer must also find the firm responsible and the price fair and reasonable (FAR 19.1406; caps as of October 2026, check the official page).

Set-asides exist so agencies can meet a statutory goal. The federal government aims to award at least 5% of all federal contracting dollars to SDVOSBs each year (SBA).

Winning a set-aside also brings work-share rules. Under 13 CFR 125.6, on contracts above the simplified acquisition threshold, you may pay no more than the following share of the contract amount to firms that are not similarly situated:

Contract typeMax paid to non-similarly-situated subcontractors
Services (except construction)50%
Supplies50% (cost of materials excluded)
General construction85%
Specialty trade construction75%

"Similarly situated" means other certified SDVOSBs. Example (illustration only): On a $1 million services set-aside, you could pay up to $500,000 to subcontractors that are not SDVOSBs.

What Are SDVOSB Set-Asides and How Do They Work?

Contracting officers use the "Rule of Two." If they reasonably expect offers from two or more eligible SDVOSBs and an award at a fair market price, they can set the contract aside for SDVOSB competition.

FAR 19.1405 states the two conditions: two or more eligible SDVOSB concerns will submit offers, and the award will be made at a fair market price (FAR 19.1405). The same section lists who counts as eligible. An offeror must be "designated in SAM as an SDVOSB concern certified by SBA." The other route is closing out: it covers firms that represented SDVOSB status in SAM and applied to SBA by December 31, 2023, while their application is pending.

The Revolutionary FAR Overhaul is changing Part 19 agency by agency through class deviations. Law firm Holland & Knight's summary of the September 2025 model text says the Rule of Two survives, and that SDVOSB program rules are "substantively the same." One change matters: the old ban on SDVOSB sole-source awards for work already performed under the 8(a) program has been removed (Holland & Knight). For orders under multiple-award contracts, set-asides are now discretionary and can't be protested.

How a contracting officer decides, step by step

  1. Market research. The officer searches the SBA certification database and SAM, and often posts a sources sought notice. Responding to these notices is how you get counted. See our Sources Sought Notice: 5-Step Response Template.
  2. Rule of Two test. Are two or more capable, certified SDVOSBs likely to bid at a fair price?
  3. Set-aside or sole-source decision. If only one SDVOSB is capable and the value is under the FAR 19.1406 cap, a sole-source award is possible.
  4. Eligibility check before award. The officer confirms your SBA certification in SAM and confirms you're small under the solicitation's NAICS code.

Pitfalls and fixes

  • Pitfall: Your capabilities are invisible during market research, so the officer can't find two SDVOSBs. Fix: Keep your SAM profile and your SBA certification profile current, and send a tight capability statement. Our Capability Statement Example shows the sections buyers read first.
  • Pitfall: You're small under your main NAICS code but not under the one in the solicitation. Fix: Check the size standard for each solicitation's code. SBA ties size to the NAICS codes in your SAM profile (13 CFR 128.200).
  • Pitfall: You confuse SDVOSB set-asides with general small business set-asides. Fix: See Total vs Partial Small Business Set Aside: 2026 Checklist for how those work.

How Do SDVOSB Set-Aside Contracts Differ Across Agencies?

VA and non-VA contracts now require the same VetCert certification, but award rules differ. The VA follows VAAR Part 819's "Veterans First" priority order, while other agencies follow FAR Subpart 19.14.

At the VA, SDVOSB set-asides come first in a mandatory sequence. The order is SDVOSBs, then veteran-owned small businesses (VOSBs), then 8(a) or HUBZone, then other small business set-asides (VAAR 819.7005). The VA must set a contract aside when market research gives a "reasonable expectation" of offers from two or more eligible certified firms at a fair and reasonable price that offers the best value (VAAR 819.7006–819.7007). Firms must be "certified and listed in the SBA certification database" both when they submit an offer and at award.

Pitfalls and fixes

  • Pitfall: You quote a $7 million manufacturing sole-source to the VA because FAR allows $8.5 million. Fix: The VA cap is $5 million, so check which regulation governs the buying agency.
  • Pitfall: Your certification lapses between submitting the offer and the award date at the VA. Fix: Start recertifying as soon as the 90-day window opens.
  • Pitfall: You count on old VA CVE verification. Fix: Certification moved to SBA in 2023, so your listing must be in SBA's system now.

What Control Requirements Must an SDVOSB Meet?

The service-disabled veteran must hold the highest officer position and manage both long-term decisions and daily operations. No non-veteran can actually control the firm or have the power to control it (13 CFR 128.203).

Control is tested separately from ownership, and a firm can own the right percentages and still fail here. The regulation requires a qualifying veteran to "hold the highest officer position in the concern (usually President or Chief Executive Officer)" with managerial experience suited to the business (13 CFR 128.203). Other key points:

  • Time commitment. The veteran cannot hold outside employment that keeps them from giving the firm the time and attention needed to control it.
  • Licenses. The veteran does not need the technical license, such as a contractor's or engineer's license. They do need "ultimate managerial and supervisory control" over the people who hold it.
  • Pay. A non-veteran cannot earn more than the veteran in the top officer role unless the higher pay is commercially reasonable.
  • Board control (corporations). Veterans must control the board of directors, through 100% ownership, 51% or more with voting control, or several veterans with enforceable voting agreements.
  • Permanent and severe disability. If the veteran has a permanent and total disability rating and cannot run daily operations, a spouse or permanent caregiver may exercise control (13 CFR 128.200; 128.203).

Steps to test your control setup

  1. Read your bylaws or operating agreement for supermajority or unanimous-consent clauses. A clause that lets a minority non-veteran owner block ordinary business decisions is a red flag.
  2. Check titles. The veteran should be CEO or President, not "Managing Member" next to a non-veteran "CEO."
  3. Compare compensation. If a non-veteran earns more, write down why that's commercially reasonable.
  4. Map who signs bank documents, bids, and hiring decisions. The veteran should hold final authority.

Pitfalls and fixes

  • Pitfall: A non-veteran co-owner runs operations while the veteran works a full-time job somewhere else. Fix: The veteran needs enough time to control the firm. Reduce the outside work or restructure roles before applying.
  • Pitfall: A key subcontractor or lender has approval rights over company decisions. Fix: Limit outside parties to normal commercial protections.

Example (illustration only): A service-disabled veteran owns 51% of a construction firm, and a non-veteran owns 49% and holds the general contractor license. This can pass if the veteran is President, supervises the license holder, and can't be outvoted on ordinary decisions. It fails if the operating agreement requires the license holder's consent to bid or hire.

SDVOSB Verification vs Self-Certification: What's the Difference?

Self-certification no longer works for SDVOSB set-asides. Firms must be certified by SBA through VetCert. The only carve-out is for firms that applied by December 31, 2023 and are still waiting for a decision.

Before 2023, SDVOSBs selling to agencies other than the VA could simply claim the status in SAM. The VA ran its own verification for its contracts. The 2021 NDAA ended that split. It gave self-certified firms a one-year grace period through January 1, 2024. Firms that applied before that date kept their eligibility until SBA decided (FAR 19.1405).

The 2024 NDAA then extended the certification requirement to subcontracts and to contracts that count toward agency goals. SBA states that all veteran firms "must also be certified by SBA VetCert Program before December 22, 2024." Self-certification remained valid through that deadline for subcontracting and goaling purposes (SBA). Firms that submitted a complete application by December 22, 2024 could keep self-certifying until SBA decided (Smith Currie).

  • Pitfall: Your SAM profile still shows an old SDVOSB self-representation, and you assume you're eligible. Fix: Look up your firm in the SBA certification database. What Is a Federal Set-Aside and How Does SDVOSB Fit In?

A federal set-aside reserves a contract for one category of small business. SDVOSB is one of four SBA socioeconomic programs, alongside 8(a), WOSB, and HUBZone, and each has its own eligibility test.

All four programs require small business status and certification through the SBA Certifications portal (SBA certifications overview).

A firm can hold more than one certification, and each opens a different pool of set-asides. Example (illustration only): A service-disabled veteran who owns a firm in a HUBZone could compete for SDVOSB and HUBZone set-asides once certified in both. Mentor-protégé joint ventures can also pursue SDVOSB set-asides when the protégé qualifies (SBA).

Next steps

You can do most of the eligibility work today without paying anyone.

Do it yourself today:

  1. Open your operating agreement or bylaws and check the 51%-of-each-class test and the control clauses against 13 CFR 128.202 and 128.203.
  2. Log in to SAM.gov and confirm your registration is active and your NAICS codes are correct.
  3. Sign in to MySBA Certifications with Login.gov and start the SDVOSB application. If you're already certified, note your recertification date.

If you'd rather not do it alone:

  • APEX Accelerators (free): These DoD-funded centers offer one-on-one government contracting counseling, including help with SAM registration and certifications, at no cost (Washington APEX). They suit first-time bidders. They are a weaker fit if you need a legal opinion on a complex ownership structure, because counselors don't make eligibility decisions. Wait times vary by center.
  • Veterans Business Outreach Centers (free): SBA's VBOCs offer training and counseling at no cost to veterans, service members, and military spouses (SBA VBOC program). They're good for veterans just starting out. They are less suited to defending a protest.
  • Government contracts attorney (paid): This is the right choice if you have investor agreements, trusts, or a pending denial or protest. Fees vary by firm and aren't set officially, so ask for a written quote before you start (as of October 2026). It's probably more than you need for a simple single-owner LLC.

FAQ

What percentage of a business must a veteran own for SDVOSB?

One or more service-disabled veterans must own at least 51%, unconditionally and directly. For LLCs, that means 51% of each class of membership interest. For corporations, it means 51% of all stock and 51% of each voting class (13 CFR 128.202).

Is self-certification still allowed for SDVOSB contracts?

No. SDVOSB set-asides and sole-source awards require SBA VetCert certification. The exception is firms that applied by December 31, 2023 and are still awaiting a decision (FAR 19.1405). For subcontracting and goaling, self-certification ended December 22, 2024 (SBA).

Do I need VA disability rating proof to qualify as SDVOSB?

Yes. The qualifying owner must be identified by the VA as a service-disabled veteran with a disability rated by the VA (SBA). If SBA denies you for insufficient evidence of veteran status, you can't appeal that to OHA. Fix any VA record problems before you apply (13 CFR 128.304).

Can a non-veteran co-owner control daily operations in an SDVOSB?

No. The veteran must hold the top officer role and control both daily operations and long-term decisions. A non-veteran may hold the technical license if the veteran keeps final supervisory control. The one exception is a veteran with a permanent and total disability, where a spouse or permanent caregiver may control the firm (13 CFR 128.203).

What's the difference between VA and SBA SDVOSB set-aside rules?

Both use the same SBA VetCert certification. The VA must consider SDVOSBs first, then VOSBs, under its Veterans First Rule of Two, and caps sole-source awards at $5 million (VAAR Part 819). Other agencies follow FAR 19.14, which has no fixed priority among programs and sole-source caps of $8.5 million (manufacturing) or $5 million (FAR 19.1406).
