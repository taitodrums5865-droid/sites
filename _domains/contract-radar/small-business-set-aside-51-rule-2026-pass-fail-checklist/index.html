People who search for the "small business set aside 51 rule" are usually mixing up two separate rules. The first is the requirement that at least 51% of a company be unconditionally owned and controlled by qualifying people. It applies to the socioeconomic programs: SDVOSB, VOSB, WOSB, 8(a) and HUBZone (13 CFR 128.202). The second is the limitation on subcontracting in FAR 52.219-14. Under that rule, a prime on a services or supplies set-aside may pay no more than 50% of what the government pays it to subcontractors that aren't "similarly situated." For general construction the cap is 85%, and for special trade construction it is 75%. A plain small business set-aside doesn't test ownership at all. It asks whether you are a small, for-profit U.S. business concern under your NAICS size standard (13 CFR 121.105).

Key points

  • Plain small business set-asides have no 51% ownership test
  • Services and supplies primes may pay non-SSE subs up to 50%
  • SDVOSB set-asides now require SBA VetCert, not VA CVE

Best for: Small business owners bidding on federal set-asides

What Is a Small Business Set-Aside?

A small business set-aside is a federal contract that only small businesses may compete for. The contracting officer reserves it under FAR Part 19 after market research shows that at least two capable small firms are likely to bid. In the revised FAR Part 19 (the FAR Overhaul deviation text), that "rule of two" sits at 19.104-1(a). It requires a set-aside above the micro-purchase threshold when there is "a reasonable expectation of obtaining offers from two or more responsible small business concerns" that are "competitive in terms of fair market prices, quality, and delivery" (FAR Overhaul Part 19).

The dollar thresholds matter here. As of October 1, 2025, inflation adjustments raised the simplified acquisition threshold (SAT) from $250,000 to $350,000 and the micro-purchase threshold from $10,000 to $15,000 (Pillsbury summary of the FAR Council rule). Purchases between those two numbers are reserved for small business when two or more small firms can do the work.

To bid on a set-aside, you need to be all of the following:

  • A business concern. Under 13 CFR 121.105, that means "organized for profit, with a place of business located in the United States," and operating mainly in the U.S. or contributing to the U.S. economy.
  • Small under the size standard for the NAICS code in the solicitation, counting your affiliates (SBA size standards).
  • Registered in SAM.gov with current representations. The steps are in /sam-gov-registration-guide-7-steps-from-uei-to-active/.

A set-aside can cover the whole requirement or only part of it. The differences are in /total-vs-partial-small-business-set-aside-2026-checklist/.

Pitfall: a lot of owners assume they need to be "51% American-owned" or "51% owned by the founder" to qualify for a plain small business set-aside. That isn't in 121.105. The only percentage in that definition is a limit on joint ventures: "no more than 49 percent participation by foreign business entities." The 51% ownership figure comes from the certification programs covered below.

51% Ownership and Control Rule vs. 50% Limitation on Subcontracting: What's the Difference?

The 51% rule decides who has to own and run your company to claim a socioeconomic status. The 50% rule (FAR 52.219-14 / 13 CFR 125.6) caps how much of the contract money you can pass to other firms after award. They apply at different stages and test different things.

Here is the easy way to keep them apart. The 51% test looks at your cap table and org chart. The subcontracting test looks at your invoices. A company can be 100% owned by a service-disabled veteran and still break the subcontracting rule by sending 70% of a services contract to a large subcontractor. In the other direction, a plain small business that is 0% veteran-owned can comply perfectly with 52.219-14.

One more reason the two get blurred is that the subcontracting rule used to be measured differently. Older versions of FAR 52.219-14 tested the "cost of contract performance incurred for personnel." A FAR rule published January 17, 2024 switched the clause to SBA's "amount paid" approach (Federal Register, FAR: Limitations on Subcontracting Revisions). Some older blog posts still describe the personnel-cost method, so check the date on anything you read.

How Does the 51% Ownership and Control Requirement Work?

At least 51% of the business must be unconditionally and directly owned by one or more qualifying individuals. Those same people must also control management and daily operations. The details differ somewhat by program.

HUBZoneU.S. citizens (or certain entity owners such as tribes and ANCs)13 CFR 126.200

Using the VetCert rules as the model, these are the conditions that most often trip people up:

  1. Direct ownership. The qualifying veteran must hold the 51% "directly, and not through another business entity or trust (including an ESOP)." The one exception is a revocable living trust where the veteran is grantor, trustee and current beneficiary (13 CFR 128.202).
  2. Unconditional ownership. Unexercised stock options, convertible debt and similar rights held by non-veterans "will be treated as exercised" (13 CFR 128.202). If an investor's option would push the veteran below 51% once exercised, the firm fails today.
  3. Highest officer. "A qualifying veteran must hold the highest officer position in the concern (usually President or Chief Executive Officer)" (13 CFR 128.203).
  4. Full-time devotion. The qualifying veteran "generally must devote full time to the business concern during its normal hours of operations" (13 CFR 128.203).
  5. No outside veto over ordinary business. Minority owners may hold veto rights over extraordinary actions such as adding equity holders, dissolution, selling substantially all assets, mergers and bankruptcy (13 CFR 128.203). A minority veto over hiring, budgets or bidding decisions is a red flag.
  6. Economic independence. Non-qualifying individuals can't create relationships so dependent that "the qualifying veteran cannot exercise independent business judgment without great economic risk" (13 CFR 128.203). Lopsided loans or a single landlord-supplier-customer arrangement can cause this.

Example (illustrative): a disabled veteran owns 55% of an LLC, and a non-veteran partner owns 45%. The operating agreement says that "all decisions require unanimous consent of the members." Ownership passes, but control fails, because the 45% partner can block ordinary decisions. Rewriting the operating agreement so that unanimous consent covers only the extraordinary actions listed in 128.203 is the usual fix. Have counsel review it.

The 51% rule doesn't replace the size test. A certified SDVOSB still has to be small under the NAICS code of each solicitation. For the program-specific versions of these checks, see /sdvosb-set-aside-requirements-2026-eligibility-checklist/ and /wosb-certification-requirements-2026-checklist-and-fees/.

How Does the 50% Limitation on Subcontracting (Self-Performance) Rule Work?

The prime must keep a minimum share of the contract dollars in-house, or with similarly situated subcontractors. That share is measured against the amount the government pays the prime. It has nothing to do with ownership percentages.

The percentages, quoted from FAR 52.219-14 as of October 2026:

Special trade construction75% of amount paid, excluding cost of materialsYes$500,000 − $100,000 materials = $400,000 → max $300,000

Four mechanics decide whether you pass.

Similarly situated entities don't count against you. A first-tier subcontractor counts as similarly situated when it has the same small business program status as the prime and is small under the prime contract's NAICS code (FAR 52.219-14). On an SDVOSB set-aside, work you give another small, certified SDVOSB is treated like your own. The subcontractor has to perform with its own employees, and the exclusion ends if it loses its status (13 CFR 125.6).

Timing. Compliance is measured "by the end of the base term of the contract and then by the end of each subsequent option period," or by the end of the performance period for orders (FAR 52.219-14). Being at 60% subcontracted in month three isn't a violation yet. Ending the base year there is.

Scope. Under 13 CFR 125.6, the rule applies to small business set-asides above the SAT and to 8(a), SDVOSB, VOSB, HUBZone and WOSB/EDWOSB awards. The revised FAR Part 19 still prescribes 52.219-14 at 19.104-3(c) and points to it throughout the socioeconomic program sections (FAR Overhaul Part 19).

Supplies have a second rule. If you supply a product you didn't manufacture, the nonmanufacturer rule (clause 52.219-33, prescribed at 19.104-3(e)) applies alongside the subcontracting limit (FAR Overhaul Part 19).

Penalty. A violation carries a fine of "the greater of $500,000 or the dollar amount spent, in excess of permitted levels, by the entity on subcontractors" (13 CFR 125.6).

What's next: the FAR open-case list shows FAR Case 2021-020 (Limitations on Subcontracting) on hold pending further SBA rulemaking. Trade press reports that SBA plans to tighten the construction rules by April 1, 2027 (FedLift). Until a final rule appears in the Federal Register, the percentages above are the ones that apply.

Pitfalls and fixes:

  • Counting 1099 workers as your own staff. SBA generally treats work done by independent contractors as subcontracted. If you rely on them, model them as subcontractor dollars in your tracker (13 CFR 125.6).
  • Checking only at closeout. Track the ratio monthly so you can move work in-house before the base term ends.
  • Assuming a teammate is similarly situated because it's small. It also needs the same status. A small but non-SDVOSB sub on an SDVOSB set-aside counts against your cap.

Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business (SDVOSB) Set-Aside Rules

SDVOSB set-asides require 51% unconditional ownership and control by one or more service-disabled veterans, certified through SBA's VetCert program, plus compliance with the limitation on subcontracting.

VA's Center for Verification and Evaluation (CVE) no longer does this job. Certification moved to SBA on January 1, 2023. Self-certified SDVOSBs got a grace period: firms that applied before January 1, 2024 could keep relying on self-certification for non-VA SDVOSB set-asides until SBA decided their application (Federal Register final rule, Nov. 29, 2022; Maynard Nexsen). VA set-asides never had a grace period. SBA also states that as of December 22, 2024, veteran firms that want subcontracting and goaling credit must hold VetCert certification (SBA).

To get certified:

  1. Confirm your VA service-connected disability rating letter is on hand. SBA requires owners "rated as service-disabled by the VA" (SBA).
  2. Make sure your SAM.gov registration is active, with the same legal name, UEI and address you'll use in the application.
  3. Go to certifications.sba.gov, sign in, and start the Veteran Small Business Certification application (SBA).
  4. Upload ownership documents (operating agreement or bylaws, stock ledger, any trust documents) and control documents (officer list, meeting minutes, resumes).
  5. Before you submit, check every document against the 128.202/128.203 points above: options treated as exercised, the veteran as highest officer, and no ordinary-course veto held by others.
  6. Once you're certified, confirm your status appears in SBA's public search and that your SAM.gov representations match.

Pitfalls and fixes:

  • A minority owner with a broad veto. Narrow it to the extraordinary actions listed in 13 CFR 128.203.
  • A veteran who also holds a full-time outside job. This can fail the full-time devotion test. Document the hours, or restructure before you apply.
  • Teaming with a non-SDVOSB firm that does most of the work. This can fail 52.219-14. It can also trigger the ostensible subcontractor rule, which treats the prime and sub as affiliates (13 CFR 121.103).

Veteran-Owned Small Business (VOSB) Set-Aside Rules

Set-asides for veteran-owned firms whose owners aren't service-disabled exist mainly at the Department of Veterans Affairs, under its Veterans First program. The 51% veteran ownership and control test still applies, and the firm must be SBA-certified.

Outside VA, there's no governmentwide VOSB set-aside. SBA's own summary says "Certified VOSBs may also compete for sole-source and set-aside contracts from the VA," while SDVOSBs compete "across the federal government" (SBA).

At VA, the order is fixed by statute (38 U.S.C. 8127–8128) and by VA's acquisition regulation. The contracting officer considers SDVOSB set-asides first, then VOSB set-asides. If no acceptable SDVOSB offers arrive, the set-aside is withdrawn and may be re-set-aside for VOSBs (VAAR Subpart 819.70).

What to do as a VOSB owner:

  1. Get VetCert certification as a VOSB at certifications.sba.gov. The ownership test is the same 51% "one or more veterans" standard (13 CFR 128.202).
  2. On SAM.gov Contract Opportunities, filter by the Set Aside option for veteran-owned small business and by VA as the department.
  3. Read each VA solicitation to see whether it's SDVOSB-only or open to VOSBs. A VOSB can't bid on an SDVOSB-only set-aside.
  4. Plan your subcontracting the same way an SDVOSB would. The limitation on subcontracting covers VOSB awards too (13 CFR 125.6).

Example (illustrative): a veteran-owned janitorial firm without a disability rating sees a VA facilities-services solicitation marked SDVOSB. It can't bid as the prime. Its options are to subcontract to an SDVOSB prime (counting against that prime's 50% cap) or to watch for the same requirement if VA withdraws the SDVOSB set-aside and re-competes it for VOSBs.

A single "fail" in Part A means you shouldn't bid as the prime yet. A fail in Part B usually means restructuring the team, not walking away. Bring more labor in-house, swap a large sub for a similarly situated one, or bid as a sub to another prime.

How to run the numbers for check 8 (illustrative, services):

  1. Estimate the total you expect the government to pay you for the base year. Example: $600,000.
  2. List each subcontractor's expected payments and mark each one SSE or non-SSE.
  3. Add up the non-SSE amounts. Example: $250,000 to a large IT firm and $40,000 to independent contractors comes to $290,000.
  4. Divide by the total: $290,000 ÷ $600,000 = 48.3%. That passes, but only barely, so watch it monthly.

Common Mistakes That Trigger a Small Business Set-Aside Protest

Most size and status protests come from four mistakes:

  • confusing the two "51%" figures
  • miscounting affiliates' revenue or employees
  • leaning on a large subcontractor for the core work
  • going over the subcontracting limit without tracking it

Size is judged as of a fixed date: generally the date you submit your initial offer that includes price (13 CFR 121.404). Competitors can file a size protest within a short window after the apparent awardee is named. For negotiated procurements, that window is generally five business days (13 CFR 121.1004). That leaves you very little time to fix paperwork, so get it right before you bid.

Mistake 1: believing ownership is the only test. For example, an owner reads "51% owned and controlled" and assumes a plain small business set-aside requires it. Meanwhile the firm is too big once a sister company's receipts are counted. Fix: run the affiliation analysis under 13 CFR 121.103. Common ownership, common management, identity of interest (such as close family members in similar businesses) and negative control can all create affiliation.

Mistake 2: the ostensible subcontractor problem. If your subcontractor performs the "primary and vital" requirements, or you depend on it unusually, SBA can treat the two of you as joint venturers and combine your sizes (13 CFR 121.103). This often happens alongside a breach of 52.219-14, but it's a separate size issue that can cost you the award before performance even starts.

Mistake 3: using the wrong measurement period. Receipts-based standards use an average over the prior completed fiscal years set out in 13 CFR 121.104. Employee-based standards use the average over the period in 13 CFR 121.106. Pick the wrong period and a firm near the line can flip from small to other-than-small.

Mistake 4: status drift after certification. You sell 10% of the company to an investor with a conversion right, or the qualifying owner takes a full-time job elsewhere. Your certification may still look valid, but the 128.202/128.203 tests may no longer be met. Fix: re-run Part A of the checklist whenever ownership, officers or bylaws change.

Mistake 5: no subcontracting ledger. The rule is measured cumulatively at the end of each base term or option period (FAR 52.219-14), so a firm with no running tally finds out too late. Fix: a monthly spreadsheet with columns for invoice amount, payee, SSE yes/no and cumulative percentage.

If you've lost a size determination, the appeals process is covered in /sba-size-standards-by-naics-2026-table-steps-appeals/.

Next steps

You can do the core of this today without outside help. If your structure is complicated, there are free and paid ways to get it reviewed.

DIY today:

  1. Open your SAM.gov entity record and confirm your NAICS codes and size representations. Then check the size standard for the NAICS code in the next solicitation you plan to bid on.
  2. If you claim (or want to claim) SDVOSB, VOSB, WOSB, 8(a) or HUBZone status, read your operating agreement or bylaws against the six ownership and control conditions above. Mark every veto, option and trust.
  3. For your current or next bid, fill in Part B of the checklist with real numbers. Write down which subs you're treating as similarly situated, and why.

If you'd rather not do it alone:

  • APEX Accelerators (formerly PTACs) provide counseling on registration, certification and bid preparation through DoD-funded local centers (APEX Accelerators). They suit first-time bidders and owners on a budget. They aren't the right choice for a legal opinion on a disputed ownership structure or a pending protest, because they don't act as your attorney, and wait times depend on the local center.
  • A government contracts attorney is the better fit if you have convertible notes, trusts, a minority veto, a joint venture or an active size/status protest. Fees are usually hourly and vary widely by firm and region, so ask for an engagement estimate up front (as of October 2026; check with the firm). This is overkill for a simple single-owner LLC bidding its first services set-aside.

FAQ

What is a small business set aside?

It's a federal contract that only small businesses may compete for. The contracting officer reserves it when market research shows a reasonable expectation of offers from two or more responsible small businesses at fair market prices (FAR Overhaul Part 19, 19.104-1(a)). Purchases between $15,000 and $350,000 are generally reserved for small businesses when that test is met.

What is the 51% rule for small business?

The term usually refers to the requirement that one or more qualifying individuals (service-disabled veterans, veterans, women, or disadvantaged individuals) unconditionally own at least 51% of the company and control it. That's what it takes to claim SDVOSB, VOSB, WOSB or 8(a) status (13 CFR 128.202). It doesn't apply to plain small business set-asides. It's also separate from the 50% limitation on subcontracting.

How is the limitation on subcontracting percentage calculated?

Divide the amount you paid to subcontractors that aren't similarly situated by the amount the government paid you. For supplies and construction, subtract the cost of materials from both figures first. The result must stay at or below 50% (services and supplies), 85% (general construction) or 75% (special trade). It's measured at the end of the base term and each option period (FAR 52.219-14).

Who certifies SDVOSB status now that VA CVE is gone?

The U.S. Small Business Administration does, through its Veteran Small Business Certification (VetCert) program at certifications.sba.gov. SBA took over from VA's CVE on January 1, 2023 (Federal Register). Certification is required for VA set-asides and for SDVOSB credit governmentwide (SBA).

Can a veteran-owned business bid on SDVOSB set-asides without being service-disabled?

No, not as the prime. SDVOSB set-asides require 51% ownership and control by veterans with a VA service-connected disability rating (13 CFR 128.202). A certified VOSB can compete for VA's VOSB set-asides, which VA uses after SDVOSB set-asides (VAAR 819.70). It can also work as a subcontractor to an SDVOSB prime.
