To start subcontracting on government contracts, register in SAM.gov, finish your SBA Small Business Search profile, and go after large prime contractors whose contracts carry a FAR 52.219-9 small business subcontracting plan. Those plans require primes to set dollar goals for small, veteran-owned, service-disabled veteran-owned, HUBZone, small disadvantaged and women-owned subcontractors, and to report how they did against them. You get picked when you fill a gap in that plan: the right NAICS code, the right status, and a pitch that a prime's small business liaison can approve fast. SBA's SubNet board, its prime contractor directory and agency matchmaking events are where those primes are looking (as of October 2026).

Key points

  • Primes need a subcontracting plan above $900,000 ($2M construction)
  • eSRS.gov retired Feb 20, 2026; sub reports now go through SAM.gov
  • Plan goals are percentages of subcontract dollars, not quotas

Best for: Small businesses that want to win federal work as a subcontractor

How to Subcontract Government Contracts

To subcontract government contracts, register in SAM.gov, confirm your size under your NAICS code, then pitch primes whose contracts include FAR 52.219-9 subcontracting plans. Here are the six steps in order.

Step 1: Get an active SAM.gov registration

Primes can accept your size and status straight from SAM.gov. FAR 19.703 says a prime "may accept a subcontractor's written representations of its size and socioeconomic status" or rely on what you've entered in SAM. SBA's Small Business Search, which replaced the old Dynamic Small Business Search on July 9, 2025, only lists businesses with an active SAM registration (FedBiz Access). Our SAM.gov registration guide walks through each screen from UEI to "Active."

Step 2: Confirm your size under each NAICS code you list

Small business status is tied to a NAICS code. A firm can be small under one code and large under another. Look up each code you plan to list in SBA size standards by NAICS before you claim "small" on a prime's supplier form.

Step 3: Decide which statuses you can honestly claim

Under FAR 19.703, small business, SDB, WOSB, VOSB and SDVOSB status for subcontract goal credit can rest on your own written representation, as long as it is "current, accurate, and complete as of the date of the offer." HUBZone status is handled separately: FAR 19.703 directs that protests of a subcontractor's HUBZone status be filed under 13 CFR 126.801. Being certified still helps. For example, a WOSB-certified firm gives the prime's compliance team one less thing to check.

Step 4: Build a target list of primes that must subcontract

A subcontracting plan is required when a contract awarded to a large business is expected to exceed $900,000, or $2,000,000 for construction. Small businesses, personal services contracts and contracts performed entirely outside the U.S. are exempt (FAR 19.702, as of October 2026). These amounts went up from $750,000 and $1.5 million on October 1, 2025, as part of the FAR inflation adjustment (SmallGovCon).

SBA's Directory of Federal Government Prime Contractors with Subcontracting Plans is an Excel file. Its columns include vendor name and location, contracting agency, PSC, NAICS code, state of performance, contract number (PIID), performance dates and total contract value. The data comes from FPDS, and the page was last updated March 4, 2025, so check any contract you find against current award data.

Step 5: Match your pitch to the prime's plan

Every plan has to list the principal types of supplies and services the prime expects to subcontract. It also has to name an employee who will administer the program (FAR 19.704). That administrator, usually called the small business liaison officer, is your first contact. Lead with a one-page capability statement that shows your NAICS codes, statuses and relevant past work.

Step 6: Respond to live requests and register on primes' supplier portals

Answer SubNet postings and teaming requests before they close. Most large primes also run their own supplier registration portals, so register on the ones that match your list.

How to Find Subcontractors for Government Contracts

Primes find subcontractors through SBA SubNet, SBA's Small Business Search, agency small business offices and matchmaking events. Knowing how they search tells you how to get found.

FAR 52.219-9(e) expects primes to make an outreach effort, including using SAM, veterans service organizations and small business trade associations to find sources, and to give small firms enough time to prepare bids. FAR 19.705-7 lists "insufficient market research to identify small business sources" as one sign that a prime failed to make a good faith effort. That gives primes a documented reason to search.

For example, say a prime needs a HUBZone electrical subcontractor in Ohio. It might search Small Business Search for NAICS 238210 with the HUBZone filter and the state set to Ohio. If your keywords say "electrical" but leave out "low-voltage" or "fire alarm," a narrower keyword search can miss you. Fill the capabilities narrative with the specific words a buyer would type.

Limitation: Small Business Search only reflects what's in SAM.gov plus SBA certification data. It doesn't show past performance quality, so a profile alone rarely wins work. It is not a good fit for firms that aren't registered yet, because they won't appear at all.

Subcontracting Government Contracts: Reddit Discussions and What's Actually True

Forum threads tend to say subcontracting is easier than it is and that primes "owe" small businesses work. In reality, primes pick their subs, goals are not per-contract quotas, and payment disputes are real.

Here are claims you'll often see in discussion threads, checked against the regulations.

"The prime has to give me work because of its plan." Not quite. FAR 19.704 sets goals as "a percentage of total subcontract dollars," across all of the prime's subcontracting, not for any one vendor. The prime chooses which small businesses get those dollars. The one firm commitment tied to specific firms is a good faith effort to use the small businesses it named in its proposal (FAR 52.219-9). If you were named in a proposal and then dropped, that matters. If you were never named, the prime owes you nothing.

"Plans are meaningless; nobody enforces them." That's overstated. Failing to comply in good faith with an approved plan is "a material breach of the contract" (FAR 52.219-9). The government can also assess liquidated damages equal to "the actual dollar amount by which the contractor failed to achieve each subcontracting goal" (FAR 19.705-7). How hard any agency enforces this varies, but the tools exist.

"Primes pay subs whenever they want." Partly true in practice. The plan, though, requires primes to pay small business subs "on time and in accordance with the terms and conditions." They must also notify the contracting officer of reduced or untimely payments, which the clause defines as more than 90 days past due (FAR 52.219-9). Primes also can't stop you from discussing payment problems with the contracting officer.

"Teaming agreements guarantee work." Not true. A teaming agreement signed before award usually promises a subcontract only if the prime wins and the parties agree on terms. Read the exit clauses before you sign.

FAR 52.219-9 (January 2025 version) still names esrs.gov in its text, while SAM.gov now hosts reporting at sam.gov/esrs. Industry write-ups describe the move as a system change with the same deadlines, and say access is now tied to UEI and PIID (iQuasar).

The flow-down rule is the one most first-time subs miss. If you, as a small business, win a $1.2 million non-construction subcontract from a large prime and it offers further subcontracting, you aren't asked for a plan, because small businesses are exempt under FAR 19.702. A large-business sub in the same position would have to adopt one. Either way, read the clause list attached to your purchase order. Primes routinely flow down dozens of FAR and DFARS clauses, and you're bound by each one you accept.

Pitfall: Treating the plan as the prime's paperwork and none of your business. If the prime reports you as a WOSB in SAM.gov and you aren't, the misrepresentation is yours. FAR 19.703 protects primes that rely on representations in good faith, which leaves the risk with you.

Outsourcing Government Contracts: Is It the Same as Subcontracting?

No. "Outsourcing" is everyday business language, while federal "subcontracting" is a legal relationship governed by flow-down clauses, the limitations on subcontracting and plan reporting.

Outsourcing Government Contracts: Is It the Same as Subcontracting?. Source: SAM.gov
Informal "outsourcing"Federal subcontracting
DefinitionAny work sent outside the firmA contract under a prime contract, subject to the prime's clauses
Rules that applyYour commercial contractFlow-downs plus, on set-asides, FAR 52.219-14
ReportingNoneCounted in the prime's ISR/SSR in SAM.gov when a plan applies
Risk if mislabeledLowA small prime can break the limits on subcontracting

The term matters most when you're the small prime on a set-aside. Under FAR 52.219-14 (October 2022), you can't pay non-similarly situated subcontractors more than these shares of what the government pays you:

  • Services: 50% of the amount paid for contract performance
  • Supplies (not as a nonmanufacturer): 50%, excluding the cost of materials
  • General construction: 85%, excluding materials
  • Special trade construction: 75%, excluding materials

Work done by a similarly situated subcontractor, meaning one with the same small business status as the prime, counts toward the prime's own share. That gives you a pitch. If you're a small business subbing to a small business prime on a small business set-aside, your work doesn't eat into the prime's 50%. A large-business sub's work does. Our 51 rule checklist covers the math.

Pitfall: Calling a 1099 contractor or a staffing vendor "outsourced help" and leaving them out of your subcontracting count. If they perform contract work, their dollars probably count toward the limit. Track them on the same spreadsheet as your other subs.

Missouri Government Contracts: State and Local Subcontracting Opportunities

Missouri state contracting is separate from federal SAM.gov and SubNet. Use MissouriBUYS for state bids and Missouri's Office of Equal Opportunity (OEO) for MBE/WBE/SDVE certification.

People often assume one registration covers both. It doesn't. Here's how to get set up for Missouri state work:

  1. Register as a supplier in MissouriBUYS. The state's purchasing page tells suppliers to "register now in the new MissouriBUYS, powered by MOVERS." The system handles vendor self-registration, solicitations, the bid board and online bidding (Missouri Office of Administration).
  2. If you qualify, apply for state certification. OEO runs the MBE, WBE, SDVE and HDVE programs. MBE/WBE requires that "at least 51 percent of the business must be owned by a minority and/or a woman," who must hold the top position and control the business (OEO). There's no application fee, and the standard process for Missouri-based businesses takes about 60 to 90 days (OEO FAQs, as of October 2026). Note that certification sits with OEO, not the economic development office some guides point to.
  3. Get listed in the directory primes use. Certified firms appear in the Directory of Certified M/WBE Vendors. OEO says "both contractors and state agencies use this site as a resource in soliciting certified minority and/or female participation." State primes report their certified sub participation on the "MBE/WBE, Blind/Sheltered Workshop, and SDVE Participation Report" form (Missouri OA).
  4. Check certified-only notices. OEO says that beyond the MissouriBUYS bid board, "additional procurement opportunities are shared with certified MBE, WBE, HDVE, and SDVE vendors" (OEO).

Missouri state certification doesn't make you a federal WOSB or 8(a) firm, and federal certification doesn't automatically carry over to the state list. For federal work in Missouri, you still need SAM.gov and the federal tools above. The Missouri APEX Accelerator, which the state's purchasing page links to, helps with both.

How to Read and Respond to a Prime's SBA SubNet Listing

A SubNet listing names the prime, the work, the NAICS codes, where it will be performed and the closing date. Respond to the listed contact before closing with a matched capability statement, relevant past performance and your statuses.

As of October 2026, SubNet listings are on SBA's legacy site. You can filter by state and keyword and sort by clicking column headers (SBA SubNet). SBA's newer contracting guide says small firms can view postings by contacting subcontracting@sba.gov (SBA). If the board doesn't load, use that address. Primes don't post directly. They email subcontracting@sba.gov to get a listing up, and posts "meant only to promote your small business will be removed." So don't post an ad for your own firm.

Each listing includes the business name, project description, closing date, performance start date, place of performance (state), NAICS code(s) and a point of contact with email and phone.

How to respond:

  1. Filter by your state, then by keyword (for example, "janitorial" or "HVAC"). Sort by closing date so the soonest deadlines are at the top.
  2. Check the NAICS code against your SAM registration. If the listing says 561720 and you're registered only under 561210, add the code in SAM.gov first, provided you actually do that work.
  3. Check the place of performance and start date against your capacity. A start date six weeks out means the prime needs someone who is ready now.
  4. Email the listed contact. Use a subject line with the listing title and your status, for example: "Linen services, Oregon – WOSB, NAICS 812332."
  5. Attach a one-page capability statement. Put your UEI and CAGE code (see our CAGE code guide), statuses, two or three similar jobs with contract numbers or customer names you're allowed to share, and a direct contact.
  6. Answer every question in the posting. If it asks for bonding, insurance limits or a price range, give it. An incomplete reply is easy to skip.
  7. Follow up once before the closing date. Then get on the prime's supplier portal whatever the outcome, so you're in their system for the next need.

For example, a listing for water system construction in Montana with a near-term closing date is a poor fit for a Florida firm with no Montana license. Skip it and spend the time on listings where your NAICS code, state and timing all line up.

Limitation: SubNet carries a small number of postings compared with all subcontracting activity. Many primes fill needs through their own portals without ever posting. Treat SubNet as one lead source, next to the prime directory.

New subs usually stumble on four things: an incomplete registration, statuses they can't support, flow-down terms nobody read, and loose payment terms. Each one can cost you the award or your margin.

Next steps

Pick one or two of these and finish them today.

DIY actions for today:

  1. Open SAM.gov and check that your registration is "Active." Note the expiration date, and confirm the NAICS codes match the work you want to sub on.
  2. Download SBA's prime contractor directory. Filter by your NAICS code and state, and keep 10 primes with performance dates still running.
  3. Open the SubNet listings, filter by your state, and answer one posting that matches your NAICS code before it closes.

If you'd rather not do it yourself: APEX Accelerators, formerly PTACs, offer one-on-one counseling on registration, market research and subcontracting. Services are free or offered at a nominal cost (as of October 2026; check the official page), and the program is administered through the Department of Defense (Washington APEX; Maryland APEX). APEX suits owners who want guidance and will do the work themselves. It's a poor fit if you want someone to write and submit responses for you: counselors advise but don't run your business development, and appointment availability varies by center. Private proposal consultants will do that hands-on work. Their fees vary widely and aren't standardized, so get a written scope and price before you sign.

FAQ

What is a FAR 52.219-9 subcontracting plan?

It's the contract clause that makes a large prime set separate goals for subcontracting to small, VOSB, SDVOSB, HUBZone, SDB and WOSB firms. The prime also has to name a program administrator and report results twice a year (ISR) and once a year (SSR). It applies to contracts above $900,000, or $2 million for construction, as of October 2026 (FAR 19.702). Not complying in good faith counts as a material breach.

How do I register as a subcontractor on SBA SubNet?

Small businesses don't register on SubNet. You browse the listings and contact the prime named in each one. The board is on SBA's legacy site with state and keyword filters, and SBA also says you can ask for postings through subcontracting@sba.gov (SBA). To be found by primes, make sure you show up in Small Business Search, which requires an active SAM registration.

Do I need a small business certification to subcontract on federal contracts?

Usually not. Under FAR 19.703, primes may accept your written representation, or your SAM.gov representation, of small business, SDB, WOSB, VOSB or SDVOSB status. HUBZone status is handled separately, with protests of a subcontractor's HUBZone status filed under 13 CFR 126.801. Certification can still make a prime more comfortable picking you, and you need it to compete for certain set-aside prime contracts.

What's the difference between a subcontracting plan goal and a set-aside?

A set-aside limits who can bid on the prime contract itself, for example only small businesses. A subcontracting plan goal is a target percentage of a large prime's subcontract dollars that should go to small firms (FAR 19.704). Set-asides decide who can win the prime contract. Plan goals affect who gets the subcontracts underneath it.

Where do I find Missouri state government subcontracting opportunities?

Register on MissouriBUYS for state bids. If you qualify, get MBE, WBE or SDVE certification from Missouri's Office of Equal Opportunity. There's no application fee, and it takes about 60 to 90 days for Missouri-based firms (OEO FAQs). Certified firms are listed in the state directory that primes use to find participation partners.
