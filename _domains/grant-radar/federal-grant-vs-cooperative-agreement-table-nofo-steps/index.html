Federal Grant vs Cooperative Agreement: Table + NOFO Steps

A federal grant and a cooperative agreement both give you federal money for a public purpose. The legal difference is how involved the agency will be. Under a grant, the agency is not expected to be substantially involved in the work (31 U.S.C. 6304). Under a cooperative agreement, it is expected to take part in carrying it out (31 U.S.C. 6305). You don't choose the instrument. The agency picks it before it publishes the NOFO, so what you're really deciding is whether to apply to that NOFO, given how much control you'll share.

Key points

  • Cooperative agreements add substantial federal involvement; grants don't
  • Performance reports run quarterly to annually under 2 CFR 200.329
  • Eligibility is set by each NOFO, not by the award type

Both instruments are common. With the default filters on Simpler.Grants.gov on October 19, 2026, the Funding Instrument filter showed 969 Grant listings, 546 Cooperative Agreement listings, 26 Procurement Contract listings and 52 Other. These counts change every day.

Difference 1: Funding control and "substantial involvement"

In a cooperative agreement you still run the project, but agency staff take an active part in specific pieces of it. The Uniform Guidance definitions in 2 CFR 200.1 say a cooperative agreement "provides for substantial involvement" of the agency, and a grant agreement "does not."

What counts as substantial involvement

The DOE Office of Science describes it as a federal employee "actively assisting, guiding, coordinating, or participating in the project." Its examples include managing how resources are split across sites and taking part in the research itself. It also notes that "normal oversight and stewardship are not substantial involvement" (DOE Office of Science). NIH's examples include an NIH scientist having primary responsibility for one specific aim, building a major database for the extramural team, or joining a multi-institution collaboration. Occasional advice or occasional sample analyses don't count (NIH OIR). NOAA puts it more simply: in a cooperative agreement the agency "supports the recipient's work to achieve the award's purpose" (NOAA).

What it looks like in a real NOFO

Take NIH's PAR-26-116 (a U01, clinical trial optional) as an example. It lists the instrument as "Cooperative Agreement" in Section II, Award Information. Section VI.2 then gives the PD/PI "primary responsibility to define objectives and approaches, and to plan, conduct, analyze, and publish results." An NIH intramural scientist has primary responsibility for "a substantial portion of the proposed research." Annual progress reports are prepared with that scientist's input, and disagreements go to a three-member Dispute Resolution Panel (PAR-26-116). That's the trade-off. You gain a federal collaborator, and some of your decisions become joint ones.

Difference 2: Reporting burden

The formal reporting rules are mostly the same. The extra burden in a cooperative agreement is coordination time, which doesn't show up as extra forms. Under 2 CFR 200.329, performance reports are due no more often than quarterly and no less often than annually. Annual reports are due within 90 calendar days after the reporting period, quarterly or semiannual reports within 30 days, and the final report within 120 days after the period of performance ends. Those deadlines apply to both instruments.

An agency can require more frequent or more detailed reports only by adding a "specific condition." Before doing so, it has to tell you the reason and what you must do to get the condition removed (2 CFR 200.208). That depends on your risk profile, not on whether you hold a grant or a cooperative agreement.

When you plan staff time, budget for:

  • Steering committee or check-in meetings named in the cooperative agreement terms
  • Agency review or approval steps written into the terms, such as work plans or protocols
  • Drafting progress reports together with agency staff (as in PAR-26-116)

In practice, if a NOFO lists monthly steering committee calls, put those hours into your personnel budget. Don't assume they fit inside your normal admin time.

Difference 3: Eligibility (mostly not a difference)

Who can apply is set by the program and the NOFO, not by the instrument type. Both statutes use the same recipient language, "a State, a local government, or other recipient" (6304, 6305). Simpler.Grants.gov handles Eligibility as its own filter, separate from Funding Instrument. It has 13 options covering governments, tribes, schools, nonprofits, businesses and individuals (Simpler.Grants.gov).

The registration steps are also the same for both. If you haven't confirmed your SAM.gov registration yet, do that first using our guide on how to check SAM.gov registration status.

One real eligibility-type boundary does exist. If the government's main purpose is to buy something for its own use, the law requires a procurement contract, not either kind of assistance award (31 U.S.C. 6305).

How to check the instrument type on any NOFO (step by step)

This takes about five minutes per opportunity, and it's the quickest way to decide whether a NOFO fits your capacity.

  1. Open simpler.grants.gov/search.
  2. Under Opportunity Status, check Open (and Forecasted if you want to plan ahead).
  3. Under Funding Instrument, check Grant, Cooperative Agreement or both.
  4. Under Eligibility, check your entity type. If you can put up a match, look at Cost Sharing too.
  5. Open a listing and read the full announcement. Look for "Funding Instrument" in the Award Information section (Section II in NIH NOFOs).
  6. If it says Cooperative Agreement, find the description of agency involvement. Federal rules require it in the program description or a reference to where it lives (2 CFR 200 Appendix I). In NIH NOFOs it's Section VI.2.
  7. Write down every meeting, approval and shared task, and estimate the hours.
  8. If a role is unclear, email the program officer named in the NOFO's agency contacts section before you start writing.

Common mistakes and fixes

  • Treating a cooperative agreement as "harder to win." Nothing in the statutes says that. Fix: judge each NOFO on fit and capacity.
  • Skipping the involvement section. Then your narrative ignores the agency's planned role. Fix: describe in the application how you'll work with agency staff. Our federal proposal guide for nonprofits covers how to structure the narrative.
  • Assuming lighter compliance under a grant. Cost rules, audits and reporting intervals come from the same 2 CFR 200 sections. Fix: plan the same compliance work either way.
  • Applying to a procurement contract by mistake. Fix: check that the Funding Instrument field doesn't say Procurement Contract.

Next steps

Pick one live NOFO today and run the eight steps above. That will tell you more than reading definitions.

Do it yourself today:

  1. Run the Simpler.Grants.gov search with your Eligibility box checked and save the links to two or three matching NOFOs.
  2. For any cooperative agreement on that list, copy the agency-involvement text into a document and add up the meeting hours.
  3. Start your SF-424 early with our field-by-field SF-424 instructions.

If you'd rather have help: Small businesses can use a local Small Business Development Center. There are about 1,000 SBDCs, offering no-cost consulting and low-cost training (as of October 2026; check the official SBA page). An SBDC is a good fit for business owners who need help with funding strategy. It isn't the right choice if you need someone to write your science or program narrative, because SBDC advisors are business generalists and availability varies by center. Researchers at universities can usually go to their sponsored programs office, which is often free to faculty. Independent grant writers charge fees that vary widely, so ask for a written scope and quote before you sign.

FAQ

Is a cooperative agreement a grant?

It's a separate legal instrument, but both are federal financial assistance and both fall under 2 CFR 200. The difference is whether the agency is substantially involved in the work (2 CFR 200.1). Many people informally call both "grants."

Can I ask the agency to switch a cooperative agreement to a grant?

Not when you apply. The agency picks the instrument when it designs the program and publishes it in the NOFO. If the involvement terms don't work for you, the realistic choice is to skip that NOFO and look for a different one.

What does a "U" code mean in NIH funding?

NIH "U" activity codes such as U01 are cooperative agreements. "R" codes such as R01 are grants. NIH describes its role in a U award as "partner" and its role in an R award as "patron" (NIH OIR).

How is a cooperative agreement different from a contract?

A contract is used when the government is acquiring property or services for its own direct benefit. A cooperative agreement supports a public purpose with the agency taking part (31 U.S.C. 6305). On Simpler.Grants.gov, contracts appear under the Procurement Contract filter.
