Grants for Nonprofits Without 501c3: 4 Paths Compared

Yes, you can get grants for nonprofits without 501c3 status. Most groups do it in one of four ways: through a fiscal sponsor that already has 501(c)(3) status, by applying as a government or tribal entity, by applying while Form 1023 is pending, or by applying directly to federal programs that list "nonprofits without 501(c)(3) status" as eligible. Grants.gov really does treat nonprofits without 501(c)(3) status as their own eligibility category, separate from 501(c)(3) nonprofits. That means the deciding factor is each funding notice's eligibility list, not a general rule.

Can You Apply for Grants Without a 501(c)(3)?

Yes. Without your own 501(c)(3), you apply through someone who is eligible (a fiscal sponsor, a government body, a partner) or to programs that accept non-exempt nonprofits. The money has to go to a legal entity that the funder's rules allow.

Federal notices of funding opportunity (NOFOs) list eligible applicant types using Grants.gov categories. These categories include state, county, city or township, and special district governments; federally recognized and other Native American tribal governments; independent school districts; public and private institutions of higher education; public and Indian housing authorities; "Nonprofits having a 501(c)(3) status with the IRS, other than institutions of higher education"; nonprofits without 501(c)(3) status; and small businesses. Individuals appear too, but Grants.gov says most opportunities are for organizations, not individuals.

On the SF-424 application form, the "Type of Applicant" field includes the option "N: Nonprofit without 501C3 IRS Status (Other than Institution of Higher Education)". A non-exempt nonprofit selects that option when a NOFO allows it. Our SF-424 field-by-field guide explains the rest of the form.

Whatever your status, the applying entity has to be registered. Under 2 CFR 25.200, an applicant must be registered in SAM.gov before it submits, must include its Unique Entity ID (UEI) in the application, and must keep the registration active while an application or award is open. For a fiscally sponsored project, the sponsor registers, not you.

How to check eligibility for a specific notice

  1. Open Simpler.Grants.gov Search.
  2. Under Eligibility, check "Nonprofits non-higher education without 501(c)(3)" (or your government type), and also check "Unrestricted."
  3. Open a result and find the eligibility section of the NOFO itself. The search tag is a summary, and the NOFO text controls.
  4. Look for extra conditions such as "must be incorporated in the state," "must have audited financials," or "consortium lead must be a unit of local government."
  5. Before you write anything, confirm that the entity applying (you, your sponsor, or your partner) holds an active SAM.gov registration. Our SAM.gov status check guide shows where to look.

Common mistakes and fixes

  • Mistake: Taking a "nonprofit" eligibility tag to mean 501(c)(3) only. Fix: Read both nonprofit categories. Some notices accept either kind.
  • Mistake: Starting SAM.gov registration a week before the deadline. Fix: Register first. SAM.gov's entity registration checklist lists the documents you'll need, including your legal name and physical address exactly as your EIN records show them. Our Grants.gov vs SAM.gov order guide gives the order of steps.

What Is Fiscal Sponsorship and How Does It Work for Grants?

A fiscal sponsor is an existing 501(c)(3) that legally receives and manages grant money for your project, for a fee. That lets you apply for grants before you incorporate.

Fiscal sponsorship is a set of contracts that let a person or group carry out charitable work using a sponsor's tax-exempt status. The field's standard reference, Fiscal Sponsorship: 6 Ways to Do It Right, names six models. In practice most projects end up in one of two:

Those fee ranges come from the 2023 Fiscal Sponsor Field Scan by Social Impact Commons and the National Network of Fiscal Sponsors, as summarized here. Across all sponsors, fixed fees ranged from 5% to 12% of revenue (as of the 2023 scan; check each sponsor's current rate). Under both models the sponsor must keep discretion over how charitable funds are used. This is a legal requirement, not paperwork for its own sake.

Steps to apply for a grant through a fiscal sponsor

  1. Shortlist sponsors whose mission matches yours. Search the National Network of Fiscal Sponsors directory, or ask your local community foundation which sponsors it works with.
  2. Ask each sponsor four questions: Which model do you use? What is your fee, and does it apply to federal funds? Do you have an active SAM.gov registration and UEI? Have you managed federal awards under 2 CFR 200 before?
  3. Sign a written fiscal sponsorship agreement before you apply. It should spell out who owns intellectual property, how fast payments go out, and what happens if you leave.
  4. Confirm that the funder accepts sponsored projects. Many private foundations say so in their guidelines. For federal grants the sponsor is simply the applicant, so the sponsor's type has to match the NOFO's list.
  5. Prepare the application together. The sponsor's name, EIN, and UEI go in the applicant fields. Your project is described as the program, and the sponsor's authorized representative signs.
  6. Report through the sponsor. Federal financial reports and audits fall on the sponsor, which is one reason Model A sponsors charge more.

Example (illustration only): A neighborhood watershed group with no EIN finds an EPA environmental education notice open to 501(c)(3) nonprofits. A regional environmental nonprofit agrees to act as a Model A sponsor at a 10% fee. The sponsor submits through its own Grants.gov workspace, hires the group's coordinator as a part-time employee, and files the federal reports. The group gets no money directly. In return it gets a funded program and a track record.

Pitfalls

  • Your eligibility is the sponsor's eligibility. If the NOFO excludes the sponsor's entity type, you can't apply either, however well your project fits.
  • The fee counts against your budget. Ask whether the federal award can cover the fee as an indirect cost. Our de minimis indirect rate guide explains the 15% election a sponsor without a negotiated rate might use.
  • Using a sponsor in name only. If the sponsor doesn't actually oversee how the money is spent, it puts its own exemption at risk. Expect real review, not a rubber stamp.

Government Entity Status: Who Qualifies Without 501(c)(3)

Tribal governments, state and local agencies, and public schools or universities are eligible for most federal grants without ever filing for 501(c)(3) status. Their governmental status is what makes them eligible.

The IRS explains that "Governmental units, such as states and their political subdivisions, are not generally subject to federal income tax" because of their sovereign powers and public nature. A political subdivision gets its exemption from being governmental, not from being a charity.

Steps for a government applicant

  1. Request a governmental information letter if a private funder or partner wants proof of tax status. Call the IRS at 877-829-5500. The letter is free.
  2. Register the exact legal entity in SAM.gov. For example, "City of X" or "X County Board of Commissioners," not the parks department's nickname. Use our UEI lookup guide to see whether your government already has a UEI. Many do, often under the finance office.
  3. Select the matching government type in the SF-424 "Type of Applicant" field.

Pitfall: Community groups sometimes ask a city to apply for them and pass the money through. This works, but the government becomes the recipient and must follow subaward rules. Settle in writing who does the work and who covers any match before anyone applies.

How to Start a Nonprofit Without a 501(c)(3)

You can incorporate as a nonprofit under state law, or run as an unincorporated association, while Form 1023 is pending. Until the IRS approves you, though, donations aren't tax-deductible.

Two separate systems are involved. Your state creates the legal entity. The IRS decides whether that entity is tax-exempt. The IRS accepts exemption applications from a corporation, an unincorporated association, or a trust, so incorporating isn't strictly required. Grant applications and SAM.gov registration go more smoothly for a corporation, however.

Steps

  1. Draft your organizing document. For a corporation, that's articles of incorporation, filed with your state's Secretary of State (or its equivalent office). For an unincorporated association, it's articles of association or a constitution. The IRS requires the document to include your name, your purposes, the date it was adopted, and the signatures of at least two individuals.
  2. Include the two clauses the IRS looks for. The first limits your purposes to 501(c)(3) purposes: charitable, religious, educational, scientific, literary, testing for public safety, fostering amateur sports competition, or preventing cruelty to children or animals. The second dedicates your assets to exempt purposes if you dissolve.
  3. Get an EIN from the IRS. It's required before any exemption application. The Form 1023-EZ instructions say plainly that "All organizations must have an EIN".
  4. Adopt bylaws and seat a board. Funders and SAM.gov both expect identifiable officers.
  5. Choose your IRS form. Form 1023-EZ is open if your annual gross receipts haven't gone over and won't go over $50,000 in any of the past or next three years, and your total assets are $250,000 or less. Otherwise, file Form 1023.
  6. File on Pay.gov. Both forms must be filed electronically. On Pay.gov, create an account, search for "1023," and select the form.

Pitfalls and fixes

  • Missing dissolution clause. This is one of the most common reasons the IRS asks follow-up questions. Fix: Copy the required language from the Form 1023 instructions into your articles before you file them with the state.
  • Choosing 1023-EZ when you'll soon outgrow it. If you're expecting a $150,000 federal grant within three years, you don't meet the gross-receipts test. Fix: File the full Form 1023.

Can a Nonprofit Operate Without a 501(c)(3)?

Yes. State nonprofit incorporation and federal tax exemption are separate. You can operate legally as a state nonprofit corporation before or without 501(c)(3) status, but you give up deductible donations and many grant opportunities.

There's also a small-organization exception. A charity whose gross receipts are normally not more than $5,000 a year may be treated as 501(c)(3) without applying. That doesn't help much with grants, because it leaves you with no determination letter to show a funder.

What changes when you run without recognized 501(c)(3) status:

  • Donors: Gifts generally aren't deductible until the IRS recognizes you. If you file within 27 months, recognition can apply back to your formation date (see the pending section below).
  • Private foundations: Many grant only to organizations they can verify in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search. If you aren't listed, you're often screened out automatically.
  • Federal grants: Not ruled out. Notices that list nonprofits without 501(c)(3) status remain open to you, provided you're registered in SAM.gov.
  • Taxes: Without exemption, a nonprofit corporation may owe federal income tax on net income. Talk to a tax professional if money is building up.

Example (illustration only): A state-incorporated food pantry with an EIN but no IRS letter yet could apply directly to a USDA notice that lists "Nonprofits without 501(c)(3) status." At the same time it could raise individual donations through a Model C sponsor, so donors get a deductible receipt.

Free Grants for Nonprofits Without 501(c)(3): What's Really Available

Most offers of "free grants" are scams. The real options are fiscally sponsored projects, small grants from local community foundations, and federal notices that accept non-exempt applicants, all of which take an actual application.

USAGov warns that "Promises of 'grants' or 'free money' from the government are often scams". The FTC adds that legitimate government assistance never charges an upfront or processing fee. Red flags include an unsolicited call, text, or social media message; a request for a "processing fee"; or payment by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency. Report these offers at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

Pending 501(c)(3) Status: What Grants Can You Apply For While Waiting?

With a pending application, you can often apply using your EIN and state incorporation, because IRS approval usually dates back to your formation if you filed on time.

Here is the IRS rule. If you file Form 1023 or 1023-EZ within 27 months after the end of the month you were legally formed, and the application is approved, your exemption takes effect on your formation date. If you file later, the IRS asks extra questions, and the effective date may be your submission date instead.

Steps while you wait

  1. Save your Pay.gov submission confirmation. Some private funders accept it as proof that your application is pending.
  2. Apply directly to federal notices that list "Nonprofits without 501(c)(3) status." On the SF-424, choose "N" because that is your status today, not the status you expect to get.
  3. For 501(c)(3)-only funders, use a short-term Model C sponsor and plan to move the grants to your own entity once you're approved.
  4. Check Tax Exempt Organization Search after the benchmark date passes. If your filing predates the IRS cutoff date and you still aren't listed, call 877-829-5500.

Pitfall: Choosing "M: Nonprofit with 501C3 IRS Status" on the SF-424 while you're still pending. That misstates your status on a federal form. Fix: Choose "N" and explain in the narrative that your application is pending.

Fiscal Sponsorship vs. 501(c)(3): Which Is Right for Your Project?

Fiscal sponsorship is faster and has no IRS fee, but you give up some control and a share of your funding. Your own 501(c)(3) gives you independence and direct eligibility, but costs more upfront and can take months.

Decision rule: If your project is a pilot, will last less than two years, or has no one to handle bookkeeping, choose a sponsor. If you expect steady revenue for several years and can recruit an independent board, file Form 1023 now. Each year on a sponsor at a 10% fee hands over a share that, past a certain budget size, is more than a bookkeeper would cost.

Pitfalls:

  • A sponsor agreement with no clause for transferring assets and grants when you spin off. Fix: Negotiate the clause before you sign.
  • Assuming a funder will move an open federal award from your sponsor to your new 501(c)(3). Fix: Ask the program officer. Transfers of federal awards need agency approval and aren't automatic.

Next steps

Choose one legal path today and confirm it with a single eligibility check before you write any proposal.

Do it yourself today:

  1. On Simpler.Grants.gov, filter Eligibility to your entity type (or "Nonprofits non-higher education without 501(c)(3)" plus "Unrestricted"), and save two notices that fit your work.
  2. If you have no entity, email two sponsors from the National Network of Fiscal Sponsors directory with the four questions from the fiscal sponsorship section.
  3. If you're incorporated, confirm you have an EIN, then run the IRS 1023-EZ financial check to decide between the $275 and $600 forms.

If you'd rather not do it yourself:

  • A comprehensive (Model A) fiscal sponsor takes on accounting, payroll, and federal reporting for about 8%–12% of revenue (2023 field scan; check each sponsor's current rate). It suits projects with staff and federal awards. It's a poor fit for groups that want full control, or that will spin off within months, because transferring awards takes time.
  • A nonprofit attorney or CPA can draft your articles and file Form 1023. Fees vary widely by region and complexity, so ask for a flat quote in writing. This suits organizations with complicated programs or more than $50,000 a year in revenue. It's usually unnecessary for small groups that clearly qualify for 1023-EZ.
  • Free official help: Grants.gov support answers questions about registration and submission, but it can't tell you whether your project is eligible. Ask the program contact listed in the NOFO about eligibility.

FAQ

Can you be a nonprofit without being a 501(c)(3)?

Yes. A nonprofit is created under state law, and 501(c)(3) is a separate federal tax status from the IRS. Many nonprofits operate legally while their application is pending, and some never apply. Without recognition, though, donations generally aren't deductible and many foundations won't fund you.

Grants for nonprofits 501(c)(3): what do funders actually check?

Private funders usually look up your EIN in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search to confirm your status. Federal agencies check that your entity type matches the NOFO and that your SAM.gov registration and UEI are active, as 2 CFR 25.200 requires.

How long does it take to get 501(c)(3) status approved?

According to the IRS, 80% of Form 1023-EZ applications are decided within 22 days, and 80% of full Form 1023 applications within 191 days. Those figures apply to filings submitted before early 2026 (IRS, updated June 28, 2026). Incomplete applications take longer.

Do fiscally sponsored projects need their own EIN?

Under Model A, usually not. The project is a program of the sponsor and uses the sponsor's EIN and UEI. Under Model C, the project receives re-granted funds as its own grantee, so it typically needs an EIN. Ask your sponsor which it requires before you sign.
