There are almost no federal cash grants for veterans to start a business. The SBA says so directly: "The federal government does not provide grants to start a business" (SBA, Veteran-owned businesses). Five real options are open to veterans: research grants (SBIR/STTR) listed on Grants.gov, the VA's Veteran Readiness and Employment (VR&E) self-employment track for service-connected disabilities, small state or university veteran grants, private grants such as the Amber Grant for women, and SBA-backed loans plus VetCert contracting status. Most "veteran business grant" lists blend these together. This guide sorts out which ones are grants, which are loans, and which only give you a contracting advantage.

Key points

  • SBA: the federal government does not give grants to start a business
  • SBA Express loans to veteran-owned firms have a $0 upfront guaranty fee
  • VA VR&E self-employment can cover tools, licenses and supplies

Are There Grants for Veterans to Start a Business? (The Honest Answer)

No federal grant program gives veterans no-strings cash to open a for-profit business. On its grants page the SBA writes, "SBA does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business" (SBA, Grants). Most money labeled "veteran business grant" turns out to be a loan, a loan guarantee, a contracting preference, or a small private or state award.

The SBA does run grants. They just go to other places (SBA, Grants):

  • SBIR and STTR fund research and development that could be commercialized. These are the federal grants a veteran-owned startup can realistically win.
  • STEP goes through state agencies to help established small businesses export.
  • Community organization grants fund the nonprofits that run Boots to Business, Veterans Business Outreach Centers (VBOCs), and the Women Veteran Entrepreneurship Training Program (WVETP). The money pays for training you can use for free. It never reaches your business directly.

Government Grants for Veterans to Start a Business: What's Actually on Grants.gov

If you search Grants.gov for "veteran," you'll mostly find VA research, veteran health, and nonprofit service grants, not startup capital. Those grants go to organizations that serve veterans. The useful search for a founder is a filter by applicant type, not by the word "veteran."

Grants.gov eligibility categories include Small businesses, For profit organizations other than small businesses, and Individuals. Each one works as a search filter. "Small businesses" means firms that meet SBA size standards (Grants.gov, Grant Eligibility). Simpler.Grants.gov, the newer search tool, shows how few opportunities individuals can apply for. At the time of the search used for this guide, it listed only a few dozen individual-eligible opportunities, against hundreds for for-profit organizations (Simpler.Grants.gov Search).

How to search Grants.gov for grants your business can actually apply for

  1. Go to Grants.gov Search Grants.
  2. Under Opportunity Status, check Posted and Forecasted. Forecasted listings give you a head start of several months.
  3. Under Eligibility, check Small businesses. Leave "Individuals" unchecked unless you'll apply as a person rather than as a company.
  4. Under Category, try Science and Technology and other Research and Development, which is where most SBIR/STTR listings sit.
  5. In Keyword(s), enter terms tied to your product, such as "SBIR," "STTR," or your technology area. "Veteran" works poorly here.
  6. Open a listing and read the eligibility section of the Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO) before you do anything else.

Example (illustrative): Say a veteran is building a wearable sensor for workplace safety. Filtering by Small businesses plus a keyword like "SBIR sensor" would find relevant SBIR solicitations. A search for "veteran grant" would mostly turn up VA medical research awards that a startup can't apply for.

Pitfall: You find a grant you qualify for but can't submit. Applying as a business requires SAM.gov registration and a UEI, which can take weeks. Fix: Register first. The order is explained in Grants.gov vs SAM.gov: Registration Order in 4 Steps. Once you're registered, the full application walkthrough is in our Federal Grant Application Guide for Small Business.

VA Grants for Veterans to Start a Business: What the VA Actually Offers

The VA doesn't run a startup grant program for veteran-owned businesses. Its main business benefit is the VR&E self-employment track, which is limited to veterans with service-connected disabilities. The VA also used to verify veteran-owned firms, but that job moved to the SBA.

Facts that matter for your plan:

  • Certification moved to SBA. On January 1, 2023, the VA transferred VOSB and SDVOSB verification from its Center for Verification and Evaluation to the SBA's Veteran Small Business Certification program (VetCert). The SBA began taking applications on January 9, 2023 (IVMF, Syracuse University). Apply at certifications.sba.gov (SBA, Veteran contracting assistance programs).
  • Eligibility for certification: The business must be at least 51% owned and controlled by eligible veterans. For SDVOSB status, those owners must be rated as service-disabled by the VA (SBA).
  • The contracting goal: The FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act (P.L. 118-31, Sec. 863) raised the government-wide SDVOSB goal from 3% to "not less than 5 percent" of prime contract and subcontract dollars (Congressional Research Service, R47226).

Funding for Veterans to Start a Business: Loans, Grants, and Other Real Options

Steps to use the SBA Express veteran fee waiver

  1. Confirm the business is at least 51% owned and controlled by one or more veterans (or eligible spouses), as the SBA notice requires (SBA notice).
  2. Collect your DD-214 and a short business plan. A VBOC can review the plan for free.
  3. Use SBA's Lender Match tool on SBA.gov to find lenders that make SBA Express loans.
  4. Ask the lender in writing: "Will this be an SBA Express loan with the veteran upfront guaranty fee waived?"
  5. Compare the lender's own fees and interest rate. The waiver covers only SBA's upfront guaranty fee.

Pitfall: The lender books a standard 7(a) loan instead of an Express loan, and you lose the waiver. Fix: Ask for the program name in writing on the term sheet.

Grants for Disabled Veterans to Start a Business: Specific Programs to Check

Service-disabled veterans have two real options: the VA's VR&E self-employment track and SDVOSB set-aside contracts. Neither one deposits startup cash in your account. VR&E, though, can pay for specific approved startup items.

Under federal regulation, VR&E self-employment comes in two categories (38 CFR § 21.257, via Cornell LII):

  • Category I: For any veteran whose evaluation shows self-employment is "a suitable vocational goal." VA may provide vocational training, incidental business management training, licenses and fees, and "necessary tools and supplies for the occupation."
  • Category II: For veterans "with the most severe service-connected disability(ies)," where self-employment is the only reasonably feasible goal. These veterans get everything in Category I plus extended services.

Before approving either category, VA must conduct "a comprehensive review and analysis of the feasibility" of your business plan. That review covers market conditions, costs, financing, and coordination with the SBA (38 CFR § 21.257). VA.gov lists the requirements: a service-connected disability, an employment barrier, and enrollment in VR&E. Service members without a rating can still apply (VA.gov).

Steps to apply for the VR&E self-employment track

  1. Go to VA.gov Veteran Readiness and Employment and choose Apply for VR&E benefits (VA Form 28-1900). If you already have a rating, you can apply online.
  2. Go to the orientation and meet your Vocational Rehabilitation Counselor (VRC). Say early that you want the self-employment track.
  3. Write your business plan. The VA offers help with plan development and concept analysis (VA.gov). A VBOC can help too.
  4. Submit the plan for VA's feasibility review.
  5. If VA approves it, the items VA will provide (tools, licenses, supplies, training) are written into your rehabilitation plan.

Example (illustrative): Picture a veteran with a service-connected back injury who wants to start a home-based bookkeeping business. Under Category I, an approved plan might include accounting training, software, and a business license. VA would pay for those items under the plan. It would not hand over a lump-sum grant.

Pitfall: Buying equipment before the plan is approved and expecting VA to pay you back. Fix: Wait until the item appears in your signed rehabilitation plan.

Grants for Female Veterans to Start a Business: Where to Look

Women veterans have three places to look: SBA-funded training aimed at them, private grants open to all women-owned businesses, and contracting certifications they can hold alongside veteran status. None of these is a federal startup grant. Together they cover training, some cash, and access to government work.

Grants for Veterans to Start a Business in Texas, California, and Other States

State and local programs are where veterans are most likely to find actual small grants. Many state "veteran grants," though, go to organizations rather than to individual business owners. Check who the eligible applicant is before you spend time on an application.

Texas. The Texas Veterans Commission's Veteran Entrepreneur Program offers consulting on business plans, financing, market research, and government contracting. It also refers veterans to lenders and partners such as the SBA, SCORE, SBDCs, and VBOCs (TexVet). The TVC's Fund for Veterans' Assistance gives reimbursement grants to charities, local governments, and veterans service organizations, not to individual entrepreneurs. It has awarded over $359 million across more than 1,600 grants since 2009 (TVC Grants). For individuals, the TWU Veteran Grant listed above is a real cash option.

California. The Disabled Veteran Business Enterprise (DVBE) program, created in 1989 under Public Contract Code 10115, sets a goal of awarding 3% of state contracts to certified DVBEs. Certification requires an honorable discharge, a service-connected disability of at least 10%, and California residency (CalVet DVBE). The Department of General Services and CalVet run it together. As with VetCert, this is access to contracts, not a grant.

Steps to check any state's program

  1. Search your state's veterans affairs agency site for "entrepreneur" or "business enterprise."
  2. Look for the eligible applicants line. If it says "nonprofit," "local government," or "veterans service organization," you can't apply as a business owner.
  3. Check whether the program is a grant, a loan, or a certification, and whether it reimburses costs or pays upfront.
  4. Note the application window. Many small grants open once a year for a few weeks. The TWU window in 2026 was one month.
  5. Ask your regional VBOC which state programs are active right now.

Pitfall: Applying to a program that's already closed for the year. Fix: Put the next expected opening date on your calendar and use the gap to finish your registrations and business plan.

How to Find Grants for Veterans to Start a Business Near Me

The most reliable "near me" search runs through your regional Veterans Business Outreach Center. The SBA funds 31 VBOC organizations nationwide, and they know which local grants, competitions, and lenders are active (SBA VBOC). Generic web searches for "veteran grants near me" turn up mostly loan ads and fee-based lists.

VBOCs offer workshops, business plan review, mentorship, financial statement analysis, and specialized training in areas such as franchising and international trade. Service members, veterans, Guard and Reserve members, and military spouses can all use them (SBA VBOC).

Steps to find local help

  1. Open SBA's local assistance search at sba.gov/local-assistance and enter your ZIP code.
  2. Filter or scroll to Veterans Business Outreach Center, and note your regional center's phone number and website.
  3. Book an intake session and ask three questions: "Which grants or pitch competitions are open locally?", "Which lenders here do SBA Express veteran loans?", and "When is the next Boots to Business Reboot?"
  4. Ask for an introduction to your SBA district office's Veterans Business Development Officer (SBA).
  5. If you plan to sell to the government, ask the VBOC to walk you through VetCert.

Example (illustrative): A veteran in San Antonio who enters a ZIP code would get the South Central region's VBOC. That center could point to the TVC consulting team and to the TWU grant window. Either is more useful than a nationwide grant list.

SBA's general help line is 866-SBA-HELP (866-722-4357), Monday–Friday, 8:00 a.m.–8:00 p.m. ET (SBA).

Next steps

Start with free registrations and counseling today. Funding decisions come later, once you know which of the five options fits.

Do it yourself today:

  1. Book a free VBOC intake through the SBA local assistance search (about 10 minutes).
  2. If you have a service-connected rating, start the VR&E application on VA.gov.
  3. If SBIR or federal contracting fits your plan, look up whether your business already has a UEI with UEI Number Lookup: Free SAM.gov Search in 3 Steps, then register in SAM.gov.

If you'd rather not do it yourself: Free help comes first. VBOCs, SCORE, and SBDCs cost nothing and suit most first-time founders. If you're going after an SBIR award and want someone to write it, a private proposal consultant is the paid option. Fees are set by each consultant (as of October 2026; get a written quote). A consultant makes sense if you have a strong technology and no time to write, and doesn't make sense for a non-R&D business, where SBIR isn't a fit anyway. Be aware that no consultant can guarantee an award, and that a fee paid to a grant consultant won't buy you the "free startup grant" the SBA says doesn't exist.

FAQ

Are there any grants for veterans to start a business?

Not from the federal government as direct startup cash. The SBA states that "the federal government does not provide grants to start a business." Real grant-type options are SBIR/STTR research awards, items paid for through VA VR&E, and small state, university, or private awards such as the $5,000 TWU Veteran Grant in Texas.

Are there grants available for veterans to start a business?

Small ones, usually through states, universities, and private foundations, often with short yearly windows. Check the eligible-applicant rules, because many state veteran funds, like the Texas Fund for Veterans' Assistance, pay organizations rather than individuals. Your regional VBOC can tell you which programs are open now.

Are there business grants for veterans?

Yes, but most are aimed at specific groups or purposes rather than general startup costs. SBIR/STTR fund R&D, VR&E covers approved items for veterans with service-connected disabilities, and the Amber Grant awards $15,000 monthly grants to women-owned businesses, including those owned by veterans. For everything else, veterans typically use SBA Express loans, which carry a $0 upfront guaranty fee for veteran-owned businesses.

Free grants for veterans to start a business — do they really exist?

Free money with no strings and no competition doesn't exist. Every legitimate grant has eligibility rules, an application, and reporting requirements. Avoid any site that charges a fee to "unlock" veteran grants, and apply only through .gov sites or organizations listed by the SBA or VA.
