To find federal grants, open the free search at Simpler.Grants.gov. Filter by Opportunity status, Eligibility, Funding instrument, Agency and the date filters, then open each remaining notice of funding opportunity (NOFO) and confirm that your organization type can apply and that you can make the closing date. The search lists forecasted, open, closed and archived opportunities. The filters below usually narrow that to a short list you can actually read.

Key points

  • Simpler Search became the default Grants.gov search in early 2026
  • SAM.gov registration can take up to 10 business days to activate
  • Most NOFOs should stay open 60 days, and almost none fewer than 30

Before you search: start registration in parallel

Start your SAM.gov registration on the same day you start searching, because it can't be rushed at the deadline. Grants.gov says to allow up to 10 business days for SAM registration to become active. If your information can't be verified, it "could take weeks or months." After SAM.gov, your organization's E-Business Point of Contact creates a Grants.gov account with the same email used in SAM.gov, adds the Unique Entity ID (UEI), and assigns roles such as Authorized Organization Representative (Grants.gov).

You don't need any of this to search. You need all of it to apply. If you're unsure which comes first, the order is laid out in Grants.gov vs SAM.gov: Registration Order in 4 Steps.

The 5 steps to find federal grants on Grants.gov

The fastest way to narrow the list is to set the filters in this order: status, eligibility, instrument and agency, then dates, then a check of the NOFO itself. Since early 2026, Simpler Search has been the default search for funding opportunities, so the labels below match simpler.grants.gov. The classic search at grants.gov uses similar facets with slightly different names (Grants.gov help).

Step 1: Set Opportunity status to Open (and maybe Forecasted)

Open simpler.grants.gov/search, find the Opportunity status filter, and select Open.

Add Forecasted only if you can plan several months ahead. A forecast is a planned opportunity that isn't officially announced yet, and Grants.gov warns it is not guaranteed to become an announcement. Forecasts are useful for nonprofits and research offices that build proposals early. For a first application, Open alone keeps the list honest.

Leave out Closed and Archived unless you're studying past competitions. Archived means a historical record of closed opportunities. Reading last year's NOFO for a recurring program is a good way to prepare, but you can't apply to it.

Step 2: Choose your organization type under Eligibility

Open the Eligibility filter and tick only the box that describes the legal entity that would sign the application. This filter cuts the list more than any other for most readers. The options include Small businesses, Nonprofits non-higher education with 501(c)(3), Nonprofits non-higher education without 501(c)(3), Public and state institutions of higher education, Private institutions of higher education, Individuals, Unrestricted and Other (Simpler.Grants.gov).

Two options need care. Few listings carry the Individuals tag, so a person applying alone, with no organization behind them, will find very little. Other usually means the NOFO lists eligibility in its own text, so a listing tagged Other may or may not include you.

Pick one box first, look at the results, then add Unrestricted. Ticking several organization types at once can bring back listings that fit only one of them.

Step 3: Narrow by Funding instrument, then Agency or Category

Under Funding instrument, choose Grant and Cooperative Agreement, and leave Procurement Contract unchecked unless you sell services to the government. One listing can carry more than one instrument, so the counts shown next to each option can add up to more than the number of results.

The practical difference is how involved the agency is. A cooperative agreement provides for "substantial involvement" between the agency and the recipient, and a grant does not. Both are financial assistance, so first-time applicants can usually keep both. The trade-offs are covered in Federal Grant vs Cooperative Agreement: Table + NOFO Steps.

Next, use Agency if you already know which department funds your field, or Category if you don't. Agency is nested. The Department of Health and Human Services, for example, lists its sub-agencies under it in the search filter. Category is broader, with options such as Health and Science, Technology, and Other Research and Development. For a small business, don't expect much under Business and Commerce. SBA does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business, and most federal money for companies runs through research programs such as SBIR and STTR.

If you have a precise term, type it in the keyword box at this point, such as a program name or an Assistance Listing number. The classic Grants.gov search has a Search Tips link that explains words, phrases and operators (Grants.gov help).

Step 4: Use Posted date, Closing date range and sorting

Set Closing date range to Next 90 days or Next 120 days, then sort by Close date (Soonest) so the deadlines you could miss appear first. The closing date options are Next 7, 30, 90 and 120 days. The Posted date options run from Within the last 3 days to Within the last 60 days (Simpler.Grants.gov).

A warning goes with this step. For a first-time applicant, Next 7 days is almost always too short. If your SAM.gov registration isn't active, you can't submit in a week, so drop those results.

Use Posted date: Within the last 14 days for a weekly check. It shows only what is new since your last look. Federal rules say agencies should make funding opportunities available for at least 60 calendar days and, except in exigent circumstances, not fewer than 30. Something posted in the past two weeks usually still leaves time to prepare.

Two more tools are worth knowing. The Cost sharing filter (Yes/No) removes programs that require you to cover part of the cost. That share is the portion of project costs not paid with Federal funds. You can also sort by Award maximum (Highest) or Award minimum (Lowest) to match award size to your capacity.

Step 5: Open each NOFO and check eligibility and deadline

For every listing left, open it and confirm two things before you read anything else: who is eligible, and the closing date. The listing's summary data, which shows labels like eligible applicants, closing date, award ceiling and floor, and cost sharing (Grants.gov opportunity layout), is a starting point. The full NOFO text is what governs.

Federal rules require each opportunity to show summary information on Grants.gov, including key dates and an executive summary of 500 words or fewer. The complete announcement must include the contents listed in Appendix I. On the classic site, the attached NOFO is on the Related Documents tab, and the application forms are on the Package tab (Grants.gov help).

Check these points:

  • Eligibility: Does the text add conditions the filter can't show, such as "must be located in a rural area" or "prior award required"?
  • Deadline: Is the date a submission deadline, and in which time zone? Is a letter of intent due earlier?
  • Version history: Has the closing date been changed? The classic site keeps a Version History tab for this (Grants.gov help).

If the eligibility text excludes you, or you can't meet the current deadline (including any earlier letter of intent), close the tab and move on. For a section-by-section reading method, see NOFO Meaning: How to Read a Federal Grant Notice in 5 Steps.

Saving and repeating your search

If you search more than once a month, save the search so you don't have to set the filters again each time. Simpler.Grants.gov lets you save funding opportunities and search queries after you sign in with a Login.gov account. The roadmap notes that a Workspace dashboard added in late 2025 lets users quickly access saved searches and applications.

The classic grants.gov search also has a Save Search button that emails you about matching opportunities. It can't include Closed or Archived status (Grants.gov help). Researchers who want to sort or annotate results can use Export Detailed Data there to download a .CSV of the current results (Grants.gov help).

The full alert setup is in Grants.gov Grant Alerts: Email Setup in 4 Steps.

When Grants.gov isn't where your money is

Grants.gov lists federal discretionary opportunities. It doesn't list everything a small business owner might mean by "grant." SBA says plainly that it does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business. Instead it funds nonprofits, Resource Partners and educational organizations that provide counseling and training. It points small businesses doing scientific R&D to the SBIR and STTR programs.

If your company has a technical product to develop, start with SBIR Grants: How to Apply in 6 Steps rather than broad keyword searching. If you sell goods or services to agencies, you're looking for contracts, not grants, and Grants.gov is built around grants and other financial assistance.

Applying is also separate from searching. You can search and read on Simpler.Grants.gov, but most applicants still start and submit on the classic Grants.gov system while the newer application workflow is tested with a small number of partner agencies.

Next steps

  1. Run the search with the filters from Steps 1–4, and save it if you'll repeat it.
  2. Open the top five results and run the Step 5 check on each. Write down the opportunity number, closing date and any extra eligibility conditions.

If you'd rather not do the searching yourself, there are two kinds of help:

  • Paid grant databases. Instrumentl's Discover plan costs $299/month billed annually or $349 billed monthly, with a 14-day free trial (as of November 2026; check the official page). It suits nonprofits that apply to many funders, private and public, all year. It isn't a good fit if you only need federal grants: the federal listings are free on Grants.gov, and the pricing page doesn't describe federal-specific coverage.
  • Advising. Small Business Development Centers offer individualized business advising and technical assistance, and Women's Business Centers offer counseling and training for business owners. These suit business owners who want someone to talk through fit and readiness. They won't write a research proposal for you, and the SBA page doesn't list SBDC fees, so ask your local center.

FAQ

Is Grants.gov free to use?

Yes. Searching Grants.gov and Simpler.Grants.gov costs nothing, and you don't need an account to browse the listings. You do need a free Login.gov account to save searches and opportunities. To apply, your organization also needs SAM.gov and Grants.gov registration.

Can an individual get a federal grant?

Rarely, through Grants.gov. Few listings carry the Individuals eligibility tag in the search filter. Most federal grants go to organizations such as nonprofits, governments, universities and businesses.

Does the government give grants to start a business?

Generally no. SBA states that it does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business. It points small businesses doing scientific research and development to the SBIR and STTR programs.

What is the difference between forecasted and posted grants?

A posted (Open) opportunity is accepting applications now. A forecasted one is planned but not officially announced, and it is not guaranteed to become an announcement. Forecasts are useful for planning, but you can't apply to one until it is posted.

How long are federal grant opportunities open?

Federal rules say agencies should keep funding opportunities open for at least 60 calendar days, and generally not fewer than 30 unless there are exigent circumstances. Always check the closing date in the NOFO itself, because it can be amended.
