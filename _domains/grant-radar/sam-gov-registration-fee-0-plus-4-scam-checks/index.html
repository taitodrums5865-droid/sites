SAM.gov charges nothing to register, renew, or get a Unique Entity ID (UEI). The General Services Administration (GSA) runs SAM.gov, and it says plainly that "registration in SAM.gov is always free" and that "the government will never ask you to pay to register, update your registration, or renew" (GSA bulletin, May 2023). Any bill you see for SAM.gov comes from a private company or from a scammer, not from the federal government.

Key points

  • GSA says registration in SAM.gov is always free, renewals included
  • Paid helpers have charged $597–$599; one 2023 deck reports up to $1,500
  • Real SAM.gov emails come only from a .gov address

Best for: first-time federal grant and contract applicants

What SAM.gov actually charges (and what it doesn't)

Every action in SAM.gov costs $0: creating an account, registering an entity, getting a UEI, updating information, and renewing. The Social Security Administration said the same thing to its Ticket to Work partners in January 2026: "There is no charge for creating or renewing a SAM account if you use the SAM website" (SSA Ticket to Work bulletin, Jan. 30, 2026). GSA also warns that you never need to pay to "fix registration errors," a common hook in scam emails (GSA bulletin).

Full registration vs. UEI only

Pick based on whether you apply for money directly. SAM.gov says full registration is for entities that "apply directly for federal awards," such as contract bids or federal assistance applications, and your UEI is assigned during that process. "Unique Entity ID only" is for entities like subawardees that only report. With just an ID, you can't apply directly for federal awards (SAM.gov entity registration). Some agencies require the full version no matter what. EXIM, for example, says its applicants and participants must complete full registration even though a UEI-only path exists (EXIM UEI page).

If you apply for federal grants, you almost certainly need full registration. Our guide to Grants.gov vs SAM.gov registration order explains why SAM.gov comes first.

Renewal every 365 days

Renewal is free, but you can't skip it. SAM.gov says registration "must be renewed every 365 days to remain active," and you can update it at any time (SAM.gov). The UEI itself stays with your entity. What lapses is the registration behind it. Scammers time their emails for this window. One law firm alert describes contractors near their renewal date being sent to a page that charged more than $1,500 (Crowell & Moring). For the free renewal walkthrough, see SAM.gov Registration Renewal: 5 Steps Before It Expires.

Who charges money, and is that legal?

Private "registration assistance" companies charge money. Some are legitimate optional services. Others look so much like SAM.gov that people pay without realizing the service is free. GSA's own wording is neutral. It says some third-party companies "will offer to help register your entity in SAM.gov for a fee," and it advises checking them with the Better Business Bureau, independent references, or a search engine before you pay (GSA bulletin).

Published reports show what these services have cost:

  • Federal Contractor Registry charged $597 for "complete SAM registration services." Its "third-party registration assistance service" disclosure sat in small print below the "New Registration" and "Renew Registration" links (Truth in Advertising, Dec. 2022).
  • US Federal Contractor Registration charged $599, according to a complaint cited by NBC News. NBC also found that some of these firms bought Google ads that ranked above the official SAM.gov page (NBC News, Jan. 20, 2023).
  • A 2023 university training deck cited an average paid fee of $599, with some firms charging up to $1,500 (UTA SAM Registration 101).

GSA draws the line at impersonation: "no third party company should ever impersonate SAM.gov for any reason" (NBC News). In practice, an honest paid helper tells you up front that it is not the government and that the work is free on SAM.gov. A bad one hides both facts.

4 scam checks before you click or pay

Run these four checks on any SAM.gov message, website, or call. If any check fails, stop and go to SAM.gov yourself.

Check 1: Does the sender end in .gov?

SAM.gov reminder and status emails "will come from a .gov email address," and "if they don't end with .gov, it's not from the government" (GSA bulletin). Look at the full address, not just the display name. One firm lists "sam-registration-gov@gsasam.com" as an example of a look-alike address (Winvale). The letters "gov" appear in it, but the domain is gsasam.com.

Check 2: Does it ask for money at all?

Any request for payment is a red flag, because the government never charges for registration, updates, or renewal. Gift cards, cryptocurrency, and wire transfers are even more suspicious: "The government will not ask you to pay fees using those methods" (GSA bulletin). For example, the Rhode Island federal court warned about an email that said a registration had expired and asked for a $500 "processing fee" (U.S. District Court, D.R.I.).

Check 3: Is the website really sam.gov?

Type sam.gov into your browser yourself. Don't use email links or the top search ad. Watchdogs documented sites that showed "multiple SAM.gov logos" (Truth in Advertising), and GSA warns about "tricky" links built to look like .gov (GSA bulletin). A real logo doesn't prove a real site. Only the domain does.

Check 4: Is it rushing you, or asking for data by phone or email?

Urgency plus a request for information is the classic pattern. Washington cities received emails with subject lines like "Immediate Action Required!" and "Update your SAM.gov account before it expires," along with calls from people claiming to be federal employees (Association of Washington Cities, Mar. 2023). GSA's guidance is short: "Don't answer requests to provide your information by phone or email" (GSA bulletin). Scammers may also quote your real registration details, which are partly public, so a correct UEI in an email doesn't prove anything (Winvale).

How to register or renew for free on SAM.gov

The free path takes four stages on SAM.gov's entity registration page: About Registration, Set up a SAM.gov Account, Prepare Your Data, and Get Started (SAM.gov). Use these steps:

  1. Type the address yourself. Open a new browser tab and enter sam.gov. Check that the address bar shows sam.gov before you sign in. Then bookmark the page and use that bookmark for every later visit, including next year's renewal.
  2. Select "Sign In" and create or use your SAM.gov user account. The account belongs to a person. The registration you create later belongs to your entity.
  3. Check where you stand first. Select "Check Entity Status" to see whether your organization already has a registration or UEI. Someone before you may have created one, so check before you start a new one. Our SAM.gov status lookup guide and free UEI lookup walk through the screens.
  4. Prepare your data. For full registration, download SAM.gov's Entity Registration Checklist and gather the details it lists. For UEI only, you need your legal business name and physical address (SAM.gov). Enter your legal name and address exactly as they appear in your official records.
  5. Select "Get Started," then choose your path. Pick "Register Entity" if you'll apply directly for grants or contracts, or "Get a Unique Entity ID" if you only report as a subawardee.
  6. Submit, then watch for .gov email only. After you submit, any status messages that matter will come from a .gov address (GSA bulletin). If a .com email asks you to "finish" or "activate" your registration for a fee, delete it.
  7. Renew before day 365. When your registration approaches expiration, sign in and select "Renew Entity." The cost is still $0.

Where to get free, legitimate help

Two kinds of free help cover nearly every SAM.gov problem: the Federal Service Desk and APEX Accelerators.

The Federal Service Desk at FSD.gov is GSA's official help desk for SAM.gov questions, and GSA points users there by name (GSA bulletin). Use it for technical problems such as a stuck validation or a sign-in error.

APEX Accelerators (formerly called PTACs) give one-on-one help. SBIR.gov says every APEX Accelerator offers free, hands-on help with SAM registration, through more than 300 local offices in nearly every state plus DC, Puerto Rico, and Guam (SBIR.gov). The SBA has also run APEX webinars on registering and maintaining a SAM.gov profile (SBA event). The limit is time: appointments depend on local staff. If your deadline is days away, start on your own and bring the APEX counselor your specific error.

What to do if you already paid or clicked

If you already paid or clicked, act quickly. First, sign in to SAM.gov directly and check that your registration details haven't been changed. Then report what happened. GSA says to report phishing and other cybercrime to the Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) and to report "scams, fraud, and bad business practices" to the Federal Trade Commission (GSA bulletin). If you paid by card, call your card issuer and ask about a dispute. Card issuers set their own rules and deadlines, so do it the same day.

If you gave a login or bank details, change your SAM.gov password and tell your bank. A paid third party that really did file your registration hasn't harmed it. You just paid for work you could have done for free. Ask the company what access it still holds, and remove anyone who shouldn't have it.

Next steps

You can settle this today with three free actions:

  1. Open sam.gov yourself, select "Check Entity Status," and write down your expiration date.
  2. Search your inbox for "SAM" and delete anything that isn't from a .gov address.
  3. If your registration expires within 60 days, start "Renew Entity" now using our renewal steps.

If you'd rather not do it yourself, your best free option is an APEX Accelerator counselor (SBIR.gov). It suits first-time registrants and small nonprofits that want someone to review their entries. It doesn't suit you if your deadline is tomorrow and the local office is booked.

Paid third-party services are legal and optional. Even so, organizations with staff and procurement rules have paid for them without needing to: the Association of Washington Cities reported that member cities were charged hundreds of dollars for a service SAM.gov provides free (Association of Washington Cities, Mar. 2023). If your organization pays invoices centrally, tell whoever approves payments that SAM.gov never bills. If a vendor's offer reaches you, check its current price on its own page, then run it through the four scam checks above before anyone pays.

FAQ

Is there a fee to register on SAM.gov?

No. GSA says registration in SAM.gov "is always free," and the government will never ask you to pay to register, update, or renew (GSA). Any fee you see comes from a private company.

Does a UEI number cost money?

No. A UEI is issued free on SAM.gov, either during full registration or through the "Get a Unique Entity ID" option, which needs only your legal business name and physical address (SAM.gov). Companies that sell "UEI numbers" are charging for a free government step.

Do I still need a DUNS number to register on SAM.gov?

No. On April 4, 2022, the federal government stopped using the DUNS number as the official entity identifier and switched to the Unique Entity ID, a 12-character alphanumeric ID generated in SAM.gov (GSA: Unique Entity Identifier Update). You don't need to get a DUNS number from an outside company first. If a service says you must buy one before registering, that's outdated or misleading.

Why did I get an email saying my SAM.gov registration expires and I need to pay?

It's almost certainly a scam or an aggressive marketing message, because renewal is free. Real SAM.gov emails come only from .gov addresses (GSA). Check your actual date by typing sam.gov into your browser and selecting "Check Entity Status."

How often do I have to renew SAM.gov, and does renewal cost anything?

You renew every 365 days to stay active, and renewal is free (SAM.gov). SSA confirmed in January 2026 that there's no charge to renew if you use the SAM website (SSA).

Are paid SAM registration services illegal?

Not by themselves. GSA describes them as optional and suggests vetting them through the BBB or independent references (GSA). Impersonating SAM.gov is where GSA draws the line (NBC News).
