Small business grants for startups exist at the federal level, but almost all of them pay for research and development, not for opening a business. If your startup has no revenue and no employees, the federal grants that can still accept you are SBIR and STTR Phase I awards. They're funded through 11 federal agencies, and the main thing reviewers look at is your technology, not your sales history. If you're not building new technology, start with an SBA Microloan (up to $50,000) or your state's programs. The SBA states plainly that it "does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business."

Key points

  • SBA says it does not give grants to start or expand a business
  • NSF SBIR Phase I pays up to $305,000 and needs no revenue
  • SAM.gov can take up to 10 business days, so register first

Best for: Pre-revenue founders checking which federal grants they qualify for

Choose by your stage and the kind of business you're building. Award size matters less than whether you're eligible at all.

Why each pick:

  • NSF Project Pitch. It's a short screening form, and NSF reviews it in about 3-4 weeks before you write a full proposal. That's the cheapest way to find out whether your idea fits.
  • STTR. It's built for university-linked teams. A research institution has to do at least 30% of the work.
  • Agency SBIR. These awards are matched to a specific agency topic, so you can tell quickly whether your project fits.
  • FAST partner. SBA funds these organizations specifically to help first-time SBIR applicants.
  • Microloan. Non-R&D startups can use it, and the average loan is about $13,000.
  • STEP. It's for established exporters, so a brand-new startup should hold off.

What Is a Small Business Grant for Startups?

A small business grant for startups is money you don't repay, given for a defined purpose such as R&D, exporting or training. It isn't general operating cash. Federal grants pay for a project with a scope, a budget and deliverables. Saying "I want to start a business" doesn't count as a project.

The SBA's grants page shows how narrow federal grants are. It lists four groups:

  • Manufacturing training (Empower to Grow)
  • Research and development (SBIR and STTR)
  • Exporting (STEP)
  • Grants to community organizations such as Small Business Development Centers

Most of these go to nonprofits, states or established businesses. Only the R&D programs regularly fund young, for-profit startups directly.

Example (illustrative): Say a two-founder company with no revenue has built a lab prototype of a cheaper water-quality sensor. It could fit an SBIR Phase I topic, because Phase I pays to test whether the technology works. A two-founder company planning a coffee shop has no R&D question to answer, so SBIR is the wrong program. A microloan or local program is the realistic path.

Common pitfalls:

  • Paying for "grant lists" or registration help. SAM.gov registration is free. Anyone who charges you to "unlock" a federal grant is selling something you can get for free.
  • Treating a grant as payroll for yourself. SBIR budgets can include salaries, but only for work on the funded project. On SBIR, the Principal Investigator must be primarily employed by the small business during the award.
  • Ignoring the project format. Every federal award is attached to a Notice of Funding Opportunity with set forms. Learn the structure first in Federal Grant vs Cooperative Agreement: Table + NOFO Steps.

Is There a Grant to Start a Small Business? (Myth vs Reality)

No. There's no general federal grant for starting a small business. The SBA's own wording: "SBA does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business." Federal startup money mostly comes as SBA-backed loans. The real grants (SBIR and STTR) need an innovative R&D project, not just a business plan.

Here's how common claims hold up against what the official pages say:

The lapse is worth knowing about. Agencies issued no new SBIR/STTR awards between October 2025 and April 2026. The Small Business Innovation and Economic Security Act of 2026 (S. 3971) restored the programs and added a Phase II "strategic breakthrough" award with a $30 million ceiling. If an article or tool says SBIR is closed, check its date.

How to Apply for a Small Business Grant for Startups

To apply, you register your company in SAM.gov first. Then you register in Grants.gov or the agency's own portal, and only after that do you match your project to an open solicitation and write. Registration alone can take up to 10 business days for SAM.gov. SBIR advisers recommend starting registrations 60 days before a deadline.

Steps

  1. Form a for-profit legal entity and get an EIN. SBIR and STTR exclude 501(c)(3) organizations, and the SBA Company Registry asks for your EIN.
  2. Register your entity in SAM.gov. This gives you a Unique Entity ID (UEI) and names your Electronic Business Point of Contact (EBiz POC). It's free and renews every year. To confirm it went through, follow Check SAM Registration Status: SAM.gov Lookup Guide.
  3. Create the Grants.gov account with the same email address as your SAM.gov EBiz POC. Add an organization applicant profile using your UEI. Then open the Manage Profiles tab on the My Account page and check the My Applicant Profile(s) table. There you'll see whether you hold the Authorized Organization Representative (AOR) role, which you need to submit (Grants.gov). The full order is in Grants.gov vs SAM.gov: Registration Order in 4 Steps (2026).
  4. Register in the SBA Company Registry at sbir.gov/registration. You'll get a nine-digit SBC Control ID. Download the PDF, which is named in the format SBC_123456789.pdf, because many agencies require it as an attachment (NSF).
  5. Find a matching solicitation or topic. Each of the 11 agencies runs its own program. At NSF you submit a Project Pitch first. You can do that at any time, and NSF has to invite you before you can send a Phase I proposal.
  6. Write and submit the proposal. Federal packages usually include an SF-424 cover form and a budget. See SF-424 Instructions: Field-by-Field Table and Steps (2026). For the SBIR-specific process, SBIR Grants: How to Apply in 6 Steps (2026) covers it in depth.

How to Get Funding for a Small Business Startup (Beyond Grants)

Grants are limited and competitive, so most pre-revenue startups pair any grant application with SBA microloans, 7(a) loans or local funds. For non-tech businesses, a loan is usually the only realistic federal path.

How to get a microloan:

  1. Open the SBA microlender directory and find an intermediary lender in your state.
  2. Contact that lender directly. SBA doesn't take microloan applications itself. The intermediary makes the credit decision and sets the terms.
  3. Ask what the lender requires. Many microlenders also require or provide training, so ask what's included.

Pitfalls: the 7(a) program isn't built for pre-launch founders, because applicants must already be operating. Microloan terms also differ from lender to lender, so compare at least two intermediaries if more than one serves your area.

How to Get a $5K Small Business Grant for Startups

A $5,000 grant almost never comes from a federal agency. Grants that small are usually private or corporate programs, or local micro-grants from cities, chambers of commerce and economic development groups. Look outside SBIR for this size.

Example of a non-federal program (not a government grant): The Amber Grant, run by WomensNet, gives three $15,000 grants every month to women-owned businesses. Monthly winners become eligible for one of three $75,000 year-end grants. Its page listed an October 31, 2026 deadline for a monthly round, with winners announced by the 21st of the following month. It doesn't fit men-owned businesses, and like any contest it isn't a predictable source of money. Check the official page for current eligibility and any application fee before you apply.

How to find small-dollar grants:

  1. Ask your city or county economic development office whether it runs a micro-grant or storefront grant program.
  2. Ask your local SBDC. SBA describes SBDCs as serving "existing small businesses and pre-venture entrepreneurs," and their advisers usually know which local programs are open.
  3. Check every private program's sponsor before you share bank or tax details.

Pitfalls: small grants often ask for a business plan or pitch video and get thousands of entries. Spend a few hours on each, not weeks. Never pay a fee to anyone claiming to give out government grants. Federal agencies don't charge to apply, and SAM.gov registration is free.

How to Get a New Business Grant for Startups (No Revenue, No Employees)

Most grant lists skip this. SBIR/STTR Phase I awards accept companies with no revenue and no employees, as long as the project is R&D. The eligibility rules cover size, ownership and structure. They don't mention revenue.

What SBIR.gov requires (as of October 2026):

  • A for-profit company with 500 or fewer employees. Full-time, part-time and leased workers all count.
  • At least 51% owned by U.S. citizens or permanent residents.
  • On SBIR, the PI is primarily employed by the company during the award.
  • On STTR, a U.S. nonprofit research institution performs at least 30% of the work and the small business performs at least 40% (SBIR.gov).

A company with zero employees still meets the 500-employee test. What usually decides the outcome is whether the project answers a technical question the agency cares about.

Steps for a pre-revenue team (NSF route)

  1. Read the program overview at seedfund.nsf.gov. NSF says it supports "first-time entrepreneurs from all 50 states and U.S. territories."
  2. Submit a Project Pitch. It's a short form, about 1,500 words, reviewed by a Program Director, and you'll usually hear back in 3-4 weeks.
  3. While you wait, finish SAM.gov, Grants.gov and the SBA Company Registry (Steps 2-4 in the application section above).
  4. If NSF invites you, the invitation is valid for the next two submission deadlines. Pick one and plan backward from it.
  5. Prepare a Phase I proposal of up to $305,000 for a 6-18 month project.

Example (illustrative): Say a solo founder has an EIN, a Delaware C-corp, no revenue and no hires. She could submit a Project Pitch while she's still employed elsewhere. If she's invited and wins an SBIR award, she would need to be primarily employed by her company during the award. That's why the job change should be planned before the proposal goes in.

The "SBA pilot" side: help, not cash

Two SBA programs fund the organizations around startups rather than the startups themselves. In September 2026, SBA gave more than $8 million to 50 FAST organizations, up to $180,000 each, covering 49 states and Puerto Rico. They provide training, mentoring and technical assistance to SBIR applicants. The Growth Accelerator Fund Competition funds accelerators that support STEM and R&D startups. If you're pre-revenue, getting into a FAST-supported program or a GAFC-funded accelerator is a practical way to get proposal help.

Small Business Grants for Startups by State: Texas, Florida, and California

State economic development programs exist in all three states. Most of the big-name ones aren't startup grants, though. They're loans, tax credits or grants to governments, so a pre-revenue founder in these states usually does better with federal SBIR plus local help.

What a startup in each state can do instead:

  • Texas. Apply federally (SBIR/STTR or an SBA Microloan). Revisit PDSBI once you have 3 years of operating history and 1 year of revenue.
  • Florida. The Job Growth Grant Fund may pay for a road or a training program that helps your industry, but the check goes to a city, county or college. For your own company, use SBIR, microloans and your local SBDC.
  • California. The tax credit can help a growing company. A startup with no taxable income gets little from a credit at first. Read the GO-Biz FY 2026-27 workshop deck before planning around it.

These state programs can be combined with federal awards later. A company that wins SBIR Phase I and starts hiring may become a fit for California Competes, for example.

Next steps

You can do most of the groundwork on your own today. For the parts you'd rather hand off, there are free official resources as well as paid consultants.

DIY actions for today:

  1. Start your SAM.gov entity registration. It's free and can take up to 10 business days.
  2. If you're building technology, draft a Project Pitch outline: the technical risk, the innovation, the team and the market. Plan to submit it on seedfund.nsf.gov once it's ready.
  3. If you're not building technology, find two microlenders in your state in the SBA directory and compare their terms.

If you'd rather not do it alone:

  • SCORE mentors give advice "at no cost." That works for business planning and loan prep, but a mentor may not have SBIR proposal experience.
  • SBDCs work with pre-venture entrepreneurs. They're a good fit for general startup and loan questions. Ask your local center whether it offers SBIR-specific advice.
  • FAST partner organizations are funded by SBA (up to $180,000 each in 2026) to help SBIR applicants. They suit first-time technical founders. If your business has no R&D component, they can't help much.
  • Private SBIR consultants charge fees that vary widely, so get a written quote (as of October 2026; check each provider's page). Hiring one makes sense when you have a strong technology and a tight deadline. It doesn't make sense if you haven't confirmed you're eligible yet. No consultant can guarantee an award.

FAQ

How do I get a small business grant in Texas for a startup?

Texas's best-known state fund, PDSBI, is a loan, and it requires 3 years of operating history and 1 year of revenue. A Texas startup should apply federally instead: SBIR/STTR if it does R&D, or an SBA Microloan through a Texas intermediary. Ask your local SBDC or city economic development office about micro-grants.

How do I get a grant to start a small business?

For a general "start my business" purpose, you can't get one from the federal government. SBA does not provide grants for starting a business. If your business involves new technology, you can apply for SBIR/STTR Phase I. If it doesn't, look at microloans, local micro-grants and private programs.

How do I apply for a small business grant for a startup?

Register in SAM.gov (free, up to 10 business days). Then set up Grants.gov with the same EBiz POC email and register in the SBA Company Registry to get an SBC Control ID. After that, find an open solicitation (or submit an NSF Project Pitch) and write the proposal. Start about 60 days before the deadline.

Can a startup with no revenue get an SBIR grant?

Yes. SBIR eligibility is about being a for-profit, at least 51% U.S.-owned company with 500 or fewer employees. Revenue isn't a requirement. What matters is a fundable R&D project and a PI who is primarily employed by the company during the award.
