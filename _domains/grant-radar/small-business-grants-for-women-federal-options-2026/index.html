The federal government has no general grant program that gives women money just to start or grow a small business. The SBA says directly: "SBA does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business." The federal options that do exist for women-owned businesses are research grants (SBIR/STTR), set-aside contracts under the Women-Owned Small Business (WOSB) Federal Contract Program, SBA microloans, and free or low-cost help from Women's Business Centers.

  • SBIR/STTR were reauthorized through Sept 30, 2031
  • WOSB set-asides support a 5% federal contracting goal

What federal funding actually exists for women-owned businesses

Federal money for women business owners comes through four channels, and only one of them is a true business grant.

SBIR and STTR research grants

SBIR and STTR fund research and development at small businesses. They pay "equity free funding through federal agencies" across 11 participating agencies. As of October 2026, SBIR.gov lists Phase I awards of up to $335,937 and Phase II awards of up to $2,239,578 (SBIR.gov). Each agency sets its own amounts and topics, so check every solicitation.

Status matters here. Both programs lapsed on September 30, 2025, and new awards paused. They were reauthorized on April 13, 2026, under the Small Business Innovation and Economic Security Act of 2026 (S. 3971), which runs through September 30, 2031 (Crowell & Moring; IEDC). Starting in fiscal year 2027, agencies will set their own caps on how many proposals one company can submit (Crowell & Moring).

Not for: service, retail, or restaurant businesses with no research component. Limitation: competition is strong, and the work has to match a topic an agency has published.

WOSB Federal Contract Program (contracts, not grants)

The WOSB program limits competition for certain federal contracts to certified women-owned firms. The government's goal is to award 5% of federal contracting dollars to these businesses (SBA).

Eligibility (SBA; 13 CFR Part 127):

  • The business is small under SBA size standards.
  • It is at least 51% owned and controlled by women who are U.S. citizens.
  • Women manage day-to-day operations and make long-term decisions.
  • EDWOSB (economically disadvantaged) has extra limits for each qualifying owner: personal net worth under $850,000, adjusted gross income of $400,000 or less averaged over three years, and personal assets of $6.5 million or less.

Not for: businesses that don't want to sell to government agencies. Limitation: certification doesn't guarantee any contract. You still have to find and win bids.

SBA Microloans (loan, not grant)

Microloans go up to $50,000 and average about $13,000. Terms run up to seven years, and interest rates are generally 8% to 13%, depending on the intermediary lender (as of October 2026; check the official page) (SBA). You can use the money for working capital, inventory, supplies, and equipment. You can't use it to pay existing debts or buy real estate.

Not for: anyone who needs money they don't have to repay.

Women's Business Centers and other SBA-funded help

SBA grants go to organizations that train entrepreneurs, and those organizations pass the help on to you. Women's Business Centers offer free or low-cost counseling and training. The SBA also funds the Women Veteran Entrepreneurship Training Program for women veterans, service members, and spouses (SBA Grants).

How to find and apply for a federal grant, step by step

You'll register once and then search on Grants.gov or SBIR.gov. Most federal awards require an active SAM.gov registration.

Step 1: Register your entity in SAM.gov

  1. Go to SAM.gov, sign in through Login.gov, and start the entity registration.
  2. Use your legal business name and address exactly as they appear in IRS records.
  3. Renew every 365 days. SAM.gov states: "You must renew your registration every 365 days to keep it active."

If you're already registered, confirm you're active with our guide to checking your SAM.gov registration status.

Step 2: Search Grants.gov with the right filters

  1. Open Grants.gov → Search Grants.
  2. Under Opportunity Status, check Posted, and also Forecasted if you want to see upcoming programs.
  3. Under Eligibility, change All Eligibilities to the category that fits you, such as small businesses or nonprofits.
  4. In Keyword(s), try "women," "entrepreneur," or your industry.
  5. Set the sort to Close Date (Ascending) so the nearest deadlines show first.

Step 3: For R&D, check SBIR.gov solicitations

Open SBIR.gov, filter open topics by agency, and read the agency's own solicitation for page limits and amounts. Rules for FY2027 may change because of the new submission caps (Crowell & Moring).

Step 4: For WOSB, apply through SBA Certifications

  1. Confirm your primary NAICS code and your size standard.
  2. Make sure your SAM.gov profile is active.
  3. Go to SBA Certifications, use the pre-application checklists, and upload your documents, including proof of citizenship (SBA).
  4. Update your SAM.gov profile every year. SBA does a program examination every three years.

You can also get certified through an SBA-approved third-party certifier: the El Paso Hispanic Chamber of Commerce, the National Women Business Owners Corporation, the U.S. Women's Chamber of Commerce, or the Women's Business Enterprise National Council. These certifiers may charge fees (SBA).

For a fuller walkthrough of application packages, see How to Apply for Grants for Small Business (2026 Guide).

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Most rejections and delays come from eligibility mismatches and paperwork problems, not from weak ideas.

What nonprofits and researchers should know

For nonprofits, the federal money aimed at women entrepreneurs usually goes to organizations that serve them, not to the business owners themselves. The SBA funds counseling and training organizations rather than individual businesses. That includes Women's Business Centers, PRIME (Program for Investors in Microentrepreneurs), SCORE, and SBDCs (SBA Grants). If your nonprofit runs or wants to run this kind of program, use the Eligibility filter on Grants.gov to find nonprofit opportunities and watch for SBA notices of funding opportunity. Our federal proposal guide for nonprofits covers narratives, budgets, and evaluation sections.

Researchers at universities usually take part in STTR by partnering with a small business. In STTR, the small business is the applicant (SBIR.gov).

Next steps

You can make real progress today without paying anyone.

Do it yourself today:

  1. Check or start your SAM.gov registration (SAM.gov).
  2. Run one Grants.gov search with Posted and Forecasted checked and the Eligibility filter set to your category.
  3. If you sell to government agencies, open SBA Certifications and read the WOSB checklist to see whether you qualify.

If you'd rather not do it alone:

  • Women's Business Center (free to low-cost): good for first-time applicants who want help with a business plan or loan package. It's less useful if you need deep technical help writing an SBIR proposal. Counseling availability depends on the local center (SBA).
  • SBA-approved third-party WOSB certifiers (fees vary; as of October 2026, check each certifier's official page): good if you also want a private-sector women-owned certification. It's not needed if you only want federal WOSB status, because you can apply directly through SBA Certifications (SBA).
  • SBDCs and SCORE (SBA resource partners): good for general business and finance mentoring. They're less specialized than a paid proposal consultant for complex R&D bids (SBA Grants).

FAQ

Does the federal government give free grants to women to start a business?

No, not as a general program. The SBA states that it does not give grants to start or expand a business (SBA). Federal options include SBIR/STTR for R&D, WOSB contracting, and microloans.

Are SBIR grants available again in 2026?

Yes. SBIR and STTR were reauthorized on April 13, 2026, through September 30, 2031, after a six-month lapse (Crowell & Moring). Check each agency's solicitation for its current open topics.

What is the difference between WOSB and EDWOSB?

Both require at least 51% ownership and control by women who are U.S. citizens. EDWOSB adds economic limits for each owner: net worth under $850,000, average AGI of $400,000 or less, and personal assets of $6.5 million or less (SBA).

How much can I borrow with an SBA microloan?

Up to $50,000, with an average of about $13,000 and terms of up to seven years (SBA). Rates generally run 8% to 13%, depending on the lender.

Is it safe to pay a website to find women's business grants?

You don't need to. Federal grant listings are public on Grants.gov and SBIR.gov. Be careful with any service that promises a guaranteed grant.
