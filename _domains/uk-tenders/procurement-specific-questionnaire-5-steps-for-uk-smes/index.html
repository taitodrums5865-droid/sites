The procurement specific questionnaire (PSQ) is the Cabinet Office's standard template for checking supplier suitability in procurements run under the Procurement Act 2023. It replaces the old Selection Questionnaire (SQ) that was used under the Public Contracts Regulations 2015 (Procurement Pathway). Many SME guides get one point wrong. The line between the PSQ and the SQ depends on which law the procurement runs under. It has nothing to do with contract value. Below threshold, a buyer cannot shortlist suppliers on suitability at all (section 85). It can still ask PSQ-style questions when it assesses tenders. So a small supplier has the same job at any value: register once on Find a Tender, keep a bank of reusable answers, and keep your evidence current.

Key points

  • PSQ is the Cabinet Office template for Procurement Act bids
  • Below £12,000/£30,000 buyers cannot shortlist on suitability
  • Core supplier info is shared from Find a Tender with a share code

Best for: UK SMEs, sole traders and VCSEs bidding for public contracts

What is a procurement specific questionnaire (PSQ)?

A PSQ is a standard questionnaire that a contracting authority uses for one specific procurement. It collects a supplier's core information, exclusion details and answers to conditions of participation. The Cabinet Office publishes it as "PA23_Procurement_Specific_Questionnaire_V2.0" on the Procurement Pathway. It says the PSQ "will fulfil a similar role to the Standard Selection Questionnaire" used under the 2015 regulations (Procurement Pathway).

It is called "procurement specific" because the reusable parts of your profile live somewhere else. Under the Act you enter your core supplier information once on the Central Digital Platform (CDP), which is the enhanced Find a Tender service. The PSQ then asks only the questions that relate to this particular contract. Government guidance says an authority must get confirmation before a supplier takes part in a covered procurement. It must confirm that the supplier has registered on the platform and submitted its core supplier information (GOV.UK CDP guidance).

Key dates and figures (as of November 2026):

  • The Act applies to procurements started on or after 24 February 2025. Anything started earlier carries on under the old rules (GOV.UK short guide for suppliers).
  • From 1 January 2026 the goods and services thresholds are £135,018 for central government and £207,720 for sub-central bodies such as councils. Both figures include VAT (Bird & Bird; Osborne Clarke).

A common mistake is to treat a buyer's PSQ as the official form, word for word. Many councils adapt it. Thurrock Council, for example, uses its PSQ to create a shortlist. It asks about health and safety, equality policies and insurance levels (Thurrock Council). Read each buyer's version as written.

Procurement specific questionnaire vs Selection Questionnaire: what is the difference?

The PSQ belongs to the Procurement Act 2023. The Selection Questionnaire belongs to the Public Contracts Regulations 2015. The difference is the legal regime, not the contract value. In practice, the two forms differ in how mandatory each one is and in where your basic company data comes from.

Does the Procurement Act 2023 (PA23) require a procurement specific questionnaire?

No. PA23 does not require the PSQ itself. It does require the things the PSQ collects, such as core supplier information and exclusion checks for covered procurements. Below threshold, it limits how suitability can be tested.

The PSQ guidance says the PSQ is not mandated by legislation. It says authorities should consider it and incorporate it at their discretion (Find a Tender attachment). Some commercial guides say central government must use it for above-threshold contracts (BidScript). That is a policy expectation, not a statutory one.

The binding rules for small contracts are in Part 6 of the Act. Under GOV.UK's below-threshold guidance (last updated 13 July 2026):

  1. A regulated below-threshold contract starts at £12,000 including VAT for central government bodies, NHS Trusts and NHS Foundation Trusts. For all other contracting authorities it starts at £30,000 including VAT (GOV.UK). These are the figures the rest of this guide refers to.
  2. The authority "cannot restrict the submission of tenders by reference to suppliers' suitability to perform the contract". Suitability covers legal and financial capacity and technical ability (section 85).
  3. The authority can still assess suitability at the award stage. It can use CDP data if you are registered (GOV.UK).
  4. Section 85 sets two exceptions to the ban (section 85):
  5. Larger works contracts. This covers a works contract that is below the works threshold but valued at or above the goods and services threshold for that type of authority. Those are the £135,018 and £207,720 figures above, so such a contract can still have a selection stage. Section 85 deliberately uses the goods and services figures here, not the much higher works threshold.
  6. Framework call-offs.
  7. Before inviting tenders, the authority must "consider the barriers SMEs may face" and whether it can reduce them (section 86).

The common mistake here is to assume that "no pre-qualification" means "no questions". In practice a council can put a short PSQ section inside the quote pack and mark it pass/fail at award, so your answers still need to be ready. A buyer might try to shortlist you out of a £40,000 council quote on turnover before reading your bid. If that happens, you can politely point it to section 85 and the GOV.UK guidance.

What questions are in a standard procurement specific questionnaire?

The standard PSQ starts with a short set of preliminary questions and then has three parts (Tussell; Find a Tender attachment):

  • confirmation of your core supplier information
  • additional exclusions information
  • conditions of participation set by the buyer

Here is what to expect in each part, in the order you will usually meet it.

  1. Preliminary questions. Your organisation name and your unique identifier from the Central Digital Platform. You will usually also be asked for your share code.
  2. Part 1: Core supplier information. You enter this once on Find a Tender and keep it up to date, rather than retyping it each time. It covers basic details, connected persons, financial information and exclusion declarations (Tussell; BidScript).
  3. Part 2: Additional exclusions. You give the following (Tussell):
  4. any exclusion grounds that apply to you and your connected persons
  5. details of any incident, and any mitigating steps you have taken
  6. answers for any supplier you rely on to meet the conditions
  7. a list of intended subcontractors, though the buyer may let you supply it later

The Act splits exclusion grounds into mandatory and discretionary. Mandatory grounds include convictions for offences such as fraud or bribery. Discretionary grounds include insolvency and poor past performance (BidScript).

  1. Part 3A: Conditions of participation (standard). These are the buyer's tests of legal and financial capacity and technical ability. Some are for information only and others are scored or pass/fail (Tussell). Typical asks are accounts, insurance certificates and relevant contract examples.
  2. Part 3B: Central government questions. These usually apply to central government departments, their executive agencies and non-departmental public bodies. The Cabinet Office added Procurement Policy Note (PPN) questions here in a later revision (Find a Tender attachment). A council PSQ may leave Part 3B out entirely.

Two practical points come up again and again.

  • Financial tests vary from buyer to buyer. One commercial guide quotes minimum turnover of "typically 2× contract value" and up to three years of accounts (BidScript). That is a market rule of thumb, not a Cabinet Office figure, so read the buyer's own threshold.
  • The modern slavery statement is only compulsory at £36 million turnover (BidScript). Below that, a sole trader or small charity answers "not applicable" and explains its approach briefly, rather than inventing a statement.

Procurement specific questionnaire example: what does a good answer look like?

A good PSQ answer is short and factual. Back it with evidence: a named contract, a figure and a document you can attach. The three pairs below are made-up examples showing the pattern. None of them comes from a real bid.

Example 1: insurance (Part 3A)

Weak: "We have all the insurance needed for this kind of work."

Strong: "Public liability £5m, policy no. [ref], insurer [name], expires 31 March 2027. Certificate attached as Doc 04. We will raise employer's liability to the £10m stated in the specification before the contract starts; broker letter attached as Doc 05."

The weak version gives the assessor nothing to tick. The strong version meets the buyer's stated level, gives an expiry date and offers a fix for the gap. Before you rely on a commitment like that, check the buyer's wording to see whether it will accept cover arranged before the contract starts.

Example 2: relevant experience (Part 3A)

Weak: "We have years of experience working with councils and charities."

Strong: "Grounds maintenance for [Parish Council], 1 April 2024 to 31 March 2026, £18,500 a year, 14 sites, weekly visits. Contact: [name, role, email]. Similar in scope because it covers public open space and play areas, as in your Lot 2."

Example 3: exclusions (Part 2)

Weak: (left blank because "it doesn't apply")

Strong: "None of the mandatory or discretionary exclusion grounds apply to [Company] or its connected persons listed in our core supplier information (share code provided). No intended subcontractors at this stage."

A blank exclusion box is one of the easiest ways to get marked non-compliant. Something may apply, such as a past county court judgment that has since been settled. In that case, describe the incident and the steps you took afterwards. The Act lets you explain mitigation, but only if you actually write it down. Procurement specific questionnaire template: how to build reusable answers and an evidence pack

Keep one reusable answer document that follows the PSQ V2.0 structure, plus one evidence folder with dated files. Review both every quarter and whenever your Find a Tender details change.

Here is how to set it up.

  1. Download the current PSQ from the Procurement Pathway template page. The page tells you to use "the most recent version uploaded on the tool".
  2. Create a document named "PSQ answer bank – v[date]". Use one heading per part: Preliminary, Part 1 (core information summary), Part 2, Part 3A, Part 3B.
  3. Under each heading, paste your model answer, its word count and the file name of the evidence that supports it.
  4. Make a folder with the subfolders listed in the table below. Put the expiry date at the start of every file name, for example "2027-03-31_PL_certificate.pdf".
  5. Add a review date to your calendar every three months. Add another after any change to directors, owners or address, because those changes affect your core supplier information too.

Avoid these three pitfalls:

  • Naming the wrong buyer. Assessors spot a reused answer that names another buyer straight away. Keep placeholders like [Buyer] in square brackets.
  • Attaching an expired insurance certificate. The date in the file name prevents this.
  • Letting answers drift away from your Find a Tender record. If your PSQ lists a director who is not among the connected persons in your core information, the buyer will ask why.

How to prepare for a below-threshold bid in 5 steps

There are five steps: confirm the contract is below threshold, read the notice and any PSQ, gather your evidence, draft answers against each question, then check and submit before the deadline.

  1. Confirm the regime and value. In the notice, find the estimated value. Check whether the notice says "Procurement Act 2023" or "Public Contracts Regulations 2015". If the value falls in the regulated below-threshold band set out in the PA23 section above, section 85 applies (GOV.UK). Watch for the two exceptions described there: framework call-offs, and works contracts at or above the goods and services threshold.
  2. Read the notice and the questionnaire. List every question with its word limit. Note whether each one is pass/fail, scored or for information only. Note how the buyer wants your share code: pasted into its e-tendering portal, emailed as a file, or entered another way (Telford & Wrekin supplier guide).
  3. Gather the evidence. Pull the matching files from your evidence folder. Then log in to Find a Tender and check that your supplier information is current. Select Share my information, then Confirm and get share code. If something has changed, update it first and generate a new code (Telford & Wrekin supplier guide). Our share code guide walks through those screens.
  4. Draft against each question. Start from your answer bank and rewrite each answer for this buyer's wording. Keep the order of the question, so if it asks for "scope, value, dates", answer in that order.
  5. Check and submit. Before you submit, check these four things:
  6. Every box is filled. Write "N/A" with a reason rather than leaving a gap.
  7. Attachments are referred to by name.
  8. The share code is the latest one.
  9. You upload a few hours before the deadline, not minutes before. Portals sometimes slow down near closing time.

At step 1, people most often miss that the contract is a call-off from a framework. In that case the framework's own rules apply, and you can usually only bid if you are already on it.

Where to find procurement specific questionnaires and below-threshold opportunities

Below-threshold opportunities under the Act are published on the Central Digital Platform, which is the Find a Tender service. Many buyers then run the actual bidding on their own e-tendering portals. GOV.UK guidance says a below-threshold tender notice must go on the platform before the contract is advertised anywhere else (GOV.UK).

There are exceptions. A buyer that only invites a closed group of suppliers, and doesn't advertise the contract in other ways, needs no tender notice. A buyer can also award to pre-selected suppliers without competition (GOV.UK). So get onto local buyers' supplier lists as well as watching notices.

After the award, the buyer must publish a contract details notice within these deadlines (GOV.UK):

  • 30 days for central government
  • 90 days for other authorities
  • 120 days for light-touch contracts

These notices show you who won similar work and at what value.

Here's how to get set up:

  1. Register your organisation and first administrator on Find a Tender. It is free (GOV.UK). Our Find a Tender registration guide covers each screen.
  2. Complete your core supplier information so Part 1 of any PSQ is already done.
  3. Search Find a Tender for your service and filter by notice type to find below-threshold tender notices. Use Contracts Finder for older procurements that started under the 2015 rules. Our Contracts Finder vs Find a Tender comparison explains which service carries what.
  4. Open a few real PSQs attached to live notices. Buyers often say their version is based on "PA23 PSQ V2". Seeing three or four of them shows you which questions come up every time.

The rules differ outside England. Wales uses its own Welsh PSQ, and Scotland may use the Single Procurement Document (Tussell). If you bid across borders, keep separate answer banks.

DIY actions for today

  1. Log in to (or register on) Find a Tender, check your core supplier information, and generate a fresh share code.
  2. Download PSQ V2.0 from the Procurement Pathway and copy its headings into a new "PSQ answer bank" document with today's date.
  3. Collect your insurance certificates, latest accounts and two contract examples into one folder, with expiry dates in the file names.

If you'd rather not do it yourself

A freelance bid writer or bid consultancy can build your answer bank and review your first PSQ. Before you commit, ask for a written quote that sets out exactly what is included.

  • When it is worth it. It suits you if you plan to bid several times a year and are short of time.
  • When it probably isn't. A one-off £15,000 quote may have a PSQ section of a single page.
  • What it can't fix. A consultant can't create evidence you don't have. If you have no relevant contract examples yet, no writer can fix that.

Try free help first. GOV.UK's Procurement Act guidance and the Find a Tender supplier guides cost nothing, and many councils publish their own PSQ explainers.

FAQ

What is the Cabinet Office procurement specific questionnaire?

It is the standard PSQ template, currently V2.0, that the Cabinet Office publishes on the Procurement Pathway for procurements under the Procurement Act 2023. It covers core supplier information, additional exclusions and conditions of participation. Part 3B adds extra questions for central government buyers. Authorities can adapt it to each contract.

Is the procurement specific questionnaire mandatory under PA 2023?

No. The PSQ guidance says it is not mandated by legislation and its guidance is not statutory. However, the Act does require buyers in covered procurements to check your core supplier information and exclusion grounds. Below threshold, the Act bans shortlisting on suitability (section 85), though suitability can still be assessed at award.

Which PPN covers the standard procurement specific questionnaire?

No single PPN creates the PSQ. It is a Cabinet Office template with its own guidance. PPN 03/24 covers the old Selection Questionnaire for procurements under the 2015 regulations. Questions linked to newer PPNs sit in Part 3B of the PSQ for central government buyers.

Can sole traders and charities complete a procurement specific questionnaire?

Yes. Sole traders, charities, CICs and other VCSEs can all register on Find a Tender and complete a PSQ. Some questions won't fit, such as group structure, or a modern slavery statement below £36 million turnover. For those, answer "not applicable" and give a one-line reason. Before inviting tenders for regulated below-threshold contracts, buyers must also consider the barriers SMEs face (section 86).
