In the UK, the public bid opening process is not a public event. Under the Procurement Act 2023, tenders go in through the buyer's e-tendering portal and stay locked until the deadline. After that the buyer opens them in private and works through five steps, from secure opening to award. Nobody reads your price out to the room. You can't change your bid after the deadline, but the buyer may ask you to clarify it. Once the decision is made you have a legal right to a written assessment summary.

Key points

  • UK tenders are opened privately on e-portals, with no public reading
  • Standstill lasts at least 8 working days from the award notice
  • Court claims must normally start within 30 days of knowing the facts

Best for: UK SMEs and VCSEs bidding for public contracts

What is a public bid opening?

A public bid opening is a US-style event where sealed bids are opened and read out in front of bidders. Buyers in the UK don't normally do this.

Search for "public bid opening" and most results describe American state and municipal practice. Sealed envelopes are opened at a set time, often in a public meeting, and each bidder's name and price is read aloud. That works when price is the only thing that decides the contract.

UK procurement works differently. Tenders are submitted electronically and kept unopened until the deadline. Gloucestershire County Council's contract procedure rules are a typical local authority example. They say tenders are "stored securely in the Council's e-procurement system and remain unopened until the specified date and time", and that they are then opened by an officer authorised by the Assistant Director of Legal Services (Gloucestershire CPR 12). Lancashire Fire and Rescue's standing orders call the same idea a virtual "sealed box" (Lancashire Fire and Rescue contract standing orders).

Prices also stay private because the Act doesn't pick the cheapest bid. It picks the "most advantageous tender", meaning the one that best meets the published award criteria (Procurement Act 2023, s19). The buyer can't announce a winner until it has scored quality, social value and price.

What is the public tender process?

The public tender process is the route a UK public body follows to buy competitively: planning, tender notice, submissions, evaluation, award and contract. Once the deadline has passed, five steps decide the outcome.

The Act has two competitive procedures: the open procedure and the competitive flexible procedure. Both start with a tender notice (GOV.UK competitive tendering procedures guidance). In the open procedure the minimum tendering period is 25 days when you submit electronically and all documents come with the notice. It drops to 10 days in some cases, such as when a qualifying planned procurement notice was published or there is urgency (GOV.UK Time Periods guidance).

The five post-deadline steps, in order:

  1. Secure opening. The portal releases the tenders to the buyer after the deadline. Late submissions are logged and normally excluded.
  2. Compliance checks. The buyer checks your tender is complete, that you aren't an excluded supplier, and that you meet the conditions of participation.
  3. Clarifications. The buyer may ask you to explain something that is unclear or obviously wrong. You can't use this to improve your bid.
  4. Evaluation. Evaluators score each tender against the published award criteria and weightings.
  5. Assessment summary, award notice and standstill. The buyer tells each assessed supplier the result in writing, publishes the award and waits before signing. The award and standstill sections below explain each part.

For example, a council tender for grounds maintenance might close at noon on a Friday. The compliance checks would happen the next week, clarification questions might follow a few days later, and scoring would take a few weeks after that. Every buyer sets its own timetable. The Act doesn't fix how long evaluation takes, so look for the indicative timetable in the tender documents.

Under the competitive flexible procedure, steps 3 and 4 can repeat, because there may be negotiation and several tendering rounds. See Competitive Flexible Procedure: 6 SME Steps.

What happens to your tender after the deadline?

When the deadline passes, the buyer opens the tenders through its e-tendering portal, which kept them locked until then. Late tenders are usually rejected, and the portal records who submitted and when.

Here is what happens on the supplier side, and what to do at each point:

  1. Check you got a receipt. Portals differ: depending on the system, you may see a status message, an on-screen confirmation or an email receipt. Whatever form it takes, save a screenshot or PDF with the timestamp. If the portal still shows your response as a draft or unsubmitted, assume the buyer has nothing from you.
  2. Watch the portal's messaging area. Clarification questions normally arrive through the portal's messages or correspondence tab, not by phone. Look at it every working day until the award.
  3. Keep your team available. The person who wrote the pricing schedule should be reachable, because the buyer sets the clarification deadline and it may be short.
  4. Don't contact evaluators. Stick to the named procurement contact. Lobbying anyone else can count as a breach of the instructions.

Late tenders are taken seriously. GOV.UK's guidance names late submission as an example of breaching a procedural requirement, which lets the buyer disregard the tender (GOV.UK Assessing Competitive Tenders guidance; s19(3)(d)). Buyers' own rules are usually stricter still. Gloucestershire won't open a late tender unless the lateness was the council's fault or caused by something like a natural disaster, a pandemic or a utility failure outside the bidder's control (Gloucestershire CPR 12.4(4)).

Blaming the portal rarely helps. In Inhealth Intelligence Ltd v NHS England [2023] EWHC 352 (TCC), the bidder said the Intend portal had failed. The court found the problem was human error, with a clear "file already exists" message, and said the upload would probably have worked if it had started 20 minutes earlier (Local Government Lawyer report). Set your own internal deadline at least a day early. Upload the big files first.

How do compliance checks work on a tender?

Compliance checks are pass/fail tests run before scoring. If you fail one, the buyer may not score your tender at all. The buyer checks that your submission is complete and that you are not excluded or excludable. It also checks that you meet the published conditions of participation.

The Act is strict here. A buyer must disregard a tender from a supplier that doesn't meet the conditions of participation (s19(3)(a)). It may also disregard tenders that break a procedural requirement, offer an abnormally low price, or come from a supplier that isn't from the UK or a treaty state. The official e-learning says a procedural breach should lead to disregarding the tender only if it is material, meaning that ignoring it "would put the tender at an unfair advantage" (GOV.UK e-learning Module 7).

Buyers usually check in this order:

  1. Completeness. Every mandatory document is uploaded, signed where needed, and in the required format.
  2. Exclusions. Your supplier information on the Central Digital Platform, shared with a share code, is checked for mandatory and discretionary exclusion grounds. See Find a Tender Share Code: 5 Steps.
  3. Conditions of participation. Financial standing, insurance levels, accreditations such as Cyber Essentials, and relevant experience. These are often in the Procurement Specific Questionnaire.
  4. Pass/fail award criteria. The assessment methodology has to say in advance which criteria disqualify you if failed (GOV.UK Assessing Competitive Tenders guidance). If you fail one, the buyer doesn't have to score the rest (GOV.UK Assessment Summaries guidance).

For example, suppose a tender asks for £5 million public liability insurance as a condition of participation and you only hold £2 million. Unless the documents say you can commit to raising the cover by contract start, your tender may be disregarded before anyone reads your method statement. Check every threshold against your current certificates before you bid, and ask the buyer during the clarification window if anything is unclear.

The abnormally low price check has a safeguard. Before rejecting you on that ground, the buyer must tell you and give you a reasonable chance to show you can deliver at that price. If it is satisfied, it can't reject you for that reason (s19(4)–(5)).

Can you ask for clarifications after the tender deadline?

Buyers may ask you to clarify or correct minor errors or ambiguities, but you can't rewrite your offer or change your price to improve your position. You also can't demand to see competitors' bids or prices.

The Act has no section on post-tender clarification. The working rules come from equal treatment and case law. In Optima Health v DWP [2025] EWCA Civ 127, the Court of Appeal held that the DWP should have sought clarification of obvious pricing errors on 3 of 133 service lines instead of excluding the bidder. The court also confirmed that clarification can't be used to submit a new bid or make substantial changes (Local Government Lawyer, 7 March 2025). That case was decided under the old 2015 Regulations, and none of the sources cited in this guide confirm whether a court will apply the same reasoning under the 2023 Act. GOV.UK guidance adds that letting a supplier fix obvious errors "may be acceptable" depending on the process set out in the tender documents (GOV.UK abnormally low tenders short guide).

When a clarification request arrives:

  1. Read exactly what is asked and answer only that.
  2. Point to where the information already is in your tender (for example, "Section 4.2, paragraph 3").
  3. Reply through the portal before the deadline stated in the request. The Act sets no minimum response time, so the buyer's request is the only deadline that counts.
  4. Don't add improvements. Evaluators may have to ignore them, and in a close contest they could put your tender at risk.

Negotiation is different. Under the competitive flexible procedure, the buyer can build negotiation or further tender rounds into the process, but only if the tender documents say so in advance.

How are tenders evaluated and scored?

Buyers score tenders against the published award criteria to find the most advantageous tender (MAT), which takes quality and social value into account as well as price. The weightings and scoring method have to be disclosed in the tender documents, so read them before you bid.

Under the Act, the most advantageous tender is the one that meets the buyer's requirements and best meets the award criteria under the assessment methodology and weightings (s19(2)). Weightings can be shown as percentages, a ranking or another clear method (GOV.UK Assessing Competitive Tenders guidance). Criteria can only be "refined" in a competitive flexible procedure, only before final tenders, and only if the documents allow it. Refining can't add new main criteria or pass/fail tests.

Social value weightings in central government are changing. PPN 002 sets a 10% minimum for in-scope contracts. Its replacement, PPN 026, applies to central government tender notices published from 1 January 2027 and sets 0% below £1m, 10% from £1m up to £5m, and 20% at £5m or more (as of November 2026; check the PPN 026 guidance). Councils and NHS bodies set their own social value weightings. More on this in Social Value in Tenders: PPN 002 Steps.

A typical evaluation runs like this:

  1. Each evaluator scores the quality answers on their own against the scoring scale (for example 0–5 or 0–100).
  2. Evaluators meet to agree a single score per question.
  3. Price is scored using the published formula.
  4. Weighted scores are added up and the buyer checks for abnormally low prices.
  5. The buyer records why it gave each score, because these reasons go into the assessment summaries.

What is a contract award notice and what do you get if you lose?

After evaluation, the buyer sends each assessed supplier its decision in an assessment summary. The summary shows your scores and the winning tender's scores and strengths. The buyer then publishes a contract award notice (UK6) on Find a Tender.

According to GOV.UK guidance, an assessment summary must include (Assessment Summaries guidance):

  • the award criteria, their weightings and the scores available
  • your score for each criterion and sub-criterion, plus your total
  • an explanation of each score that refers to what you actually wrote
  • for unsuccessful suppliers, the same scores and explanations for the most advantageous tender, with any commercially sensitive details redacted

All summaries must go out at the same time, and before the contract award notice is published (s50). The Act sets no minimum gap between the two, and buyers often publish the notice the same day. Some cases don't need a summary at all: framework call-offs, direct awards under sections 41 or 43, and suppliers knocked out before the final stage. Those suppliers should still be told in writing that they aren't going through.

The contract award notice names the winner and starts the standstill. For contracts up to £5 million it includes the number of tenders submitted and assessed, and how many came from SMEs or VCSEs. For contracts of £5 million or more it also names the unsuccessful suppliers (GOV.UK e-learning Module 7). Once the contract is signed, a UK7 contract details notice must follow within 30 days. For contracts over £5 million, a copy of the contract must be published within 90 days (s53).

To read your result:

  1. Download the assessment summary from the portal on the day it arrives.
  2. Note the standstill end date. It has to be stated in the UK6 notice.
  3. Find the notice on Find a Tender by searching the buyer's name. The notice title starts "UK6: Contract award notice".
  4. Compare your explanation for each criterion with the winner's, line by line. Write down any score where the reasoning doesn't match your tender.

What is the standstill period and how long does it last?

The standstill period is a pause of at least 8 working days under the Act, between publication of the contract award notice and signing of the contract. It gives unsuccessful suppliers time to raise concerns before the contract is concluded.

Section 51 sets the mandatory period at eight working days, starting on the day the contract award notice is published (s51). GOV.UK guidance (updated 17 August 2026) says the publication day counts as working day one, so the earliest the contract can be signed is working day nine. Weekends and bank holidays in any part of the UK don't count (Contract Award Notices and Standstill guidance). This replaced the old 10 calendar days.

Example: if a UK6 notice is published on Tuesday 1 December 2026, day 1 is that Tuesday and day 8 is Thursday 10 December, so the contract can't be signed before Friday 11 December. No bank holiday falls inside that window.

A notice published over Christmas shows how bank holidays stretch the count. If a UK6 notice is published on Thursday 24 December 2026, that Thursday is day 1. Christmas Day (Friday 25 December) and the substitute Boxing Day holiday (Monday 28 December) don't count. Nor do New Year's Day (Friday 1 January 2027) or Scotland's substitute 2 January holiday (Monday 4 January 2027), even for an English buyer, because holidays in any UK nation are excluded (GOV.UK bank holidays). Day 8 is then Friday 8 January 2027, and the contract can't be signed before Monday 11 January 2027. Check the bank holidays page for the dates that apply to your notice before relying on a calculated end date.

No mandatory standstill applies to:

  • framework call-offs
  • contracts awarded through a dynamic market
  • light touch contracts
  • direct awards for extreme and unavoidable urgency or to protect life
  • direct awards by private utilities

In these cases the buyer can choose a voluntary standstill of at least 8 working days (s51(3)–(5)). If you mostly win call-offs, check whether one has been offered. See also Framework Agreement How to Get On.

How do you challenge a public tender decision?

Start by asking the buyer for more information and going through your assessment summary. If you still think the rules were broken, put it in writing before standstill ends and get legal advice. Court deadlines are short, and claims can be costly.

  1. Ask the buyer in writing, through the portal, for an explanation of the specific scores you dispute. The Act doesn't give a formal right to a further debrief, but many buyers will answer.
  2. Decide quickly whether to issue a claim. If you issue court proceedings and notify the buyer during standstill, the contract is automatically suspended. The buyer can't sign until the claim is withdrawn or resolved, or the court lifts the suspension. A claim issued after standstill doesn't trigger suspension, though you can ask the court for an injunction (Contract Award Notices and Standstill guidance).
  3. Know the time limits. Most claims must start within 30 days of when you knew, or ought to have known, the facts. A court can extend this for good reason, up to three months at most. Claims to set aside a contract have a six-month backstop (s106).
  4. Use the free review route if court is out of reach. The Cabinet Office's Public Procurement Review Service (PPRS) investigates supplier concerns about a specific procurement through its online form, "Raise a supplier concern with public sector procurement, contract management, or late payment". The issue must be within the last 2 years. Its recommendations are non-binding and it can't stop an award (Procurement compliance oversight, updated 30 October 2026).

Older articles still point suppliers to the Procurement Review Unit, but GOV.UK says it "no longer exists as a business unit" from 1 April 2026. PPRS now sits in the Cabinet Office's Procurement Compliance and Oversight team (GOV.UK Procurement Review Unit page). Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own routes for devolved procurements, and Wales has a separate set of regulations (Welsh Government: Procurement reform).

Next steps

You can do the first three of these today.

  1. Open the tender documents for your live bid and write down three things: the clarification deadline, the pass/fail criteria, and the indicative award date.
  2. Check that your supplier information on the Central Digital Platform is up to date and that your share code is valid. If you're new, start with Contracts Finder vs Find a Tender.
  3. Set up a simple tracker for each bid with columns for "Submitted (timestamp)", "Clarifications", "Assessment summary received" and "Standstill ends".

If you'd rather not handle this alone, there are three realistic routes:

  • Free official training: The Cabinet Office's Procurement Act 2023 e-learning costs nothing (as of November 2026; check the official page). It's written for buyers, so it suits suppliers who want to know how evaluators think. It won't help with a specific bid.
  • A bid writer or consultant: Prices vary widely by contract size and aren't regulated, so ask for a fixed quote. This suits firms bidding often or above £1m. It's poor value for a one-off small quote.
  • A procurement solicitor: This is the only realistic route to a court challenge or automatic suspension. It suits you when a contract matters to your business and the 30-day clock is running. It doesn't suit small, low-value disputes, where the free PPRS route makes more sense.

FAQ

How long does it take to hear the result of a tender?

The Act doesn't set a fixed evaluation period, so check the indicative timetable in the tender documents and ask the named contact if it slips. If the buyer decides not to award a contract at all, it doesn't just go quiet: it must publish a procurement termination notice (UK12) on Find a Tender (Find a Tender: Notice types and sequences). Searching the buyer's notices is therefore a quick way to see whether a delayed procurement is still live.

What happens if I submit my tender late?

Your tender will very likely be disregarded. GOV.UK guidance gives late submission as an example of a procedural breach, and many buyers' rules allow exceptions only for the buyer's own fault or events outside your control. Courts have refused to blame portals for last-minute upload mistakes.

Can I get feedback if my tender is unsuccessful?

Yes. If your tender was assessed at the final stage, the Act gives you an assessment summary with your scores, the reasons for them, and the winning tender's scores and reasons. Framework call-offs and some direct awards are exempt.

Can a public body negotiate with me after I submit my tender?

Only if the procedure allows it. In the competitive flexible procedure, the buyer can include negotiation or more tender rounds if it says so in advance. In the open procedure, you can only answer clarification questions, and you can't change your price or content.

Will my competitors see my prices?

Not in full. Unsuccessful suppliers get details of the winning tender's assessment, and buyers can withhold commercially sensitive material under section 94 of the Act. Contracts over £5 million are published within 90 days of signing, which can show the agreed price.
